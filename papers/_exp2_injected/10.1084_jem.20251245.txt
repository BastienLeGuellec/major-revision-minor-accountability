TITLE: Cerebrospinal fluid and dural accumulation of α-synuclein impairs CNS clearance in Parkinson’s disease

ABSTRACT:
Impaired waste clearance and chronic inflammation are emerging contributors to Parkinson’s disease pathogenesis. Here, Stevenson et al. show that aggregated α-synuclein at CNS borders promotes TNF-driven meningeal inflammation and impairs CNS clearance, identifying these tissues as potential therapeutic targets.

BODY:
IntroductionThe central nervous system (CNS) relies on specialized clearance pathways to remove metabolic waste and maintain neurological health (Kim et al., 2025; Kipnis et al., 2025; Rustenhoven and Kipnis, 2022). Through the glymphatic system, interstitial waste from the brain parenchyma is exchanged with the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and subsequently drained through meningeal and nasal lymphatics to the cervical LNs. These pathways converge at CNS border tissues, particularly the meninges and associated lymphatic structures, which function as sites of both waste clearance and immune surveillance (Louveau et al., 2018; Mazzitelli et al., 2023; Rustenhoven et al., 2021; Rustenhoven and Kipnis, 2022; Smyth et al., 2024). Dysfunctions in these clearance pathways have been implicated in aging (Hitpass Romero et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2017; Rustenhoven et al., 2023; Yoon et al., 2024), neurological diseases including Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease (PD) (Da Mesquita et al., 2018; Da Mesquita and Rua, 2024; Ding et al., 2021; Han et al., 2025; Louveau et al., 2018; Rustenhoven et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2019), and traumatic brain injuries (Bolte et al., 2020). Notably, neuroimaging studies have demonstrated impaired meningeal lymphatic drainage in patients with idiopathic PD (Ding et al., 2021), alongside dysfunction of the glymphatic system (Rajai Firouzabadi et al., 2026), suggesting that CNS clearance pathways are broadly compromised and clinically relevant in PD.PD is defined by the pathological accumulation of the misfolded protein α-synuclein (α-syn), which contributes to dopaminergic neuronal loss and progressive motor and non-motor symptoms (Bloem et al., 2021; Braak et al., 2003; Jankovic, 2008; Schapira et al., 2017). While α-syn pathology has been extensively studied in the brain parenchyma, α-syn aggregates have also been detected in peripheral compartments including the gut (Anis et al., 2022), blood (Sui et al., 2014), and liver (Reyes et al., 2021). Further, aggregated α-syn is present in the CSF (Liu et al., 2024; Pierobon Mays et al., 2024; Poggiolini et al., 2022; Schmitz et al., 2016; Si et al., 2022; Wiseman et al., 2025) of PD patients and its detection using real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) is being widely explored to assist in the diagnosis (Poggiolini et al., 2022; Schmitz et al., 2016; Wiseman et al., 2025). However, the biological consequences of extracellular α-syn within the CSF remain poorly understood. This is important because CNS borders are increasingly recognized as neuroimmune hubs containing both innate and adaptive immune cells that regulate surveillance and clearance (Cugurra et al., 2021; Drieu et al., 2022; Mazzitelli et al., 2023; Rustenhoven et al., 2021; Schonhoff et al., 2023; Van Hove et al., 2019). Given that α-syn can engage peripheral immune populations, extracellular α-syn at these interfaces may represent a persistent inflammatory stimulus that contributes to neurodegeneration (Grozdanov et al., 2019; Lindestam Arlehamn et al., 2020; Schonhoff et al., 2023). This is particularly relevant given that enhanced α-syn clearance is widely viewed as a therapeutic goal in PD (Alfaidi et al., 2025).Meningeal lymphatic vessels mediate CNS protein clearance by draining CSF solutes, including α-syn, to the deep cervical LNs (dCLNs) (Rustenhoven and Kipnis, 2022). Disruption of this pathway worsens glymphatic function, promotes α-syn accumulation, glial activation, and motor deficits in transgenic PD models (Zou et al., 2019). In parallel, α-syn drainage to dCLNs can activate macrophages and promote peripheral inflammation, while CNS α-syn exposure elevates cytokines such as IFN-γ that impair meningeal lymphatic function (Ding et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023; Rustenhoven et al., 2023). However, whether extracellular α-syn within the CSF directly acts on CNS border tissues to impair lymphatic clearance remains unclear.Here, we investigated whether extracellular α-syn aggregates at CNS borders drive functional and immunological disruption of these compartments in PD. Using a combination of human postmortem tissue analyses, seeding assays, and both acute and chronic α-syn–driven in vivo mouse models, we identify the dura mater of the meninges as a key site of α-syn deposition and inflammation from CSF α-syn aggregates. We show that α-syn disrupts CSF drainage through meningeal lymphatic vessels, promotes innate immune cell activation, and fosters a proinflammatory cytokine milieu, including elevated TNF levels. Notably, our findings demonstrate that α-syn can impair human lymphatic endothelial cell (LEC) function in vitro, both directly and indirectly through TNF-mediated signalling, highlighting a dual mechanism of lymphatic dysfunction. Together, these results reveal that α-syn accumulation at CNS interfaces is not merely a bystander but a key driver of lymphatic failure and neuroinflammation, positioning CNS border tissues as novel and actionable therapeutic targets in PD.Results and discussionThe accumulation of aggregated α-syn is well established in the CSF of PD patients, but the functional consequences of this remain unclear (Bloem et al., 2021; Wiseman et al., 2025). Recent studies have identified anatomical routes linking the CSF with CNS border tissues, including the dura mater and skull bone marrow, suggesting these sites may be directly exposed to CSF-borne α-syn aggregates (Cugurra et al., 2021; Mazzitelli et al., 2023; Smyth et al., 2024). As meningeal lymphatic vessels are enriched around the dural sinuses, and arachnoid cuff exit (ACE) points provide routes for CSF access adjacent to these regions, dural tissue was specifically sampled from these areas (Jacob et al., 2022; Louveau et al., 2015; Smyth et al., 2024) (Fig. 1, A–C). We first investigated whether aggregated α-syn is found in the dura mater in human PD. Using postmortem CSF and sinus-associated dura mater from individuals with PD and neurologically normal controls (Table S1), we detected robust seeding activity in both CSF and fresh-frozen dura tissue from PD cases by RT-QuIC, while no seeding activity was observed in neurologically normal controls (Schmitz et al., 2016; Wiseman et al., 2025) (Fig. 1, D and E). Immunohistochemical analysis of paraffin-embedded dura around the superior sagittal sinus further revealed discrete α-syn–positive inclusions in PD cases, but not in neurologically normal or Alzheimer’s disease control tissue (Fig. 1, F and G). Both intracellular and extracellular α-syn aggregates were also closely associated with Lyve1+ vessels that were weakly podocalyxin+, revealing localization near dural lymphatic-associated vascular structures (Testa et al., 2009) (Fig. 1 H). Panel V shows histograms of ovalbumin A594 fluorescence in CD45 positive cells after vehicle or alpha synuclein treatment.Given that α-syn can drive innate immune responses within the brain (Allen Reish and Standaert, 2015), we next investigated whether CSF α-syn exposure altered immune responses at CNS borders. Spectral flow cytometry analysis of the dura following acute (96 h) α-syn PFF exposure revealed a significant increase in macrophages, relative to vehicle controls (Fig. 2, J and K). In parallel, immune profiling of the skull bone marrow showed alterations in innate immune populations, including increased neutrophils, alterations in Ly6c monocyte proportions, and expansion of bone marrow progenitor populations including hematopoietic stem cells and common monocyte progenitors (Fig. S1, I–K). These findings suggest that CSF α-syn exposure induces a coordinated innate immune response across the dura and adjacent skull bone marrow compartments that supply myeloid cells to the dura (Cugurra et al., 2021).As dural immune responses have previously been shown to impair CNS clearance pathways (Rustenhoven et al., 2023), we next assessed whether α-syn exposure altered glymphatic and lymphatic function. Mice exposed to α-syn PFFs for 96 h showed significantly reduced drainage of fluorescent tracers of varying sizes, including dextran (3 kDa) and OVA (45 kDa), but not cadaverine (0.6 kDa) to the dCLNs (Fig. 2, L–O). There were no significant changes in dCLNs node size (Fig. 2 P). In this acute model, there were no significant differences in the different-sized tracer coverage throughout the brain parenchyma, indicating no change in glymphatic influx (Fig. S1, L–P). Uptake of OVA by dural macrophages, dendritic cells, and CD45− cells was also reduced, suggesting that α-syn exposure may alter CSF access to the dura, phagocyte uptake, or both (Fig. S1, Q–V). Additionally, we observed alterations in lymphatic vessel junction composition, with a greater shift towards button and mixed junctional patterns in the α-syn group compared with controls (Fig. 2, Q and R). As lymphatic endothelial junctions regulate vessel barrier integrity in a size-dependent manner, this junctional remodelling may reduce the ability of lymphatic vessels to retain and transport larger tracers to the dCLNs, leading to local leakage, retention, or inefficient downstream movement of macromolecules, while smaller solutes remain relatively preserved (Baluk et al., 2007; Jannaway and Scallan, 2021). Consistent with this, direct disruption of VE-cadherin in meningeal lymphatic vessels impairs CSF drainage of 45 kDa OVA to dCLNs, and altered meningeal lymphatic junctions are a feature of aging-associated lymphatic dysfunction (Ahn et al., 2019; Da Mesquita et al., 2018; Rustenhoven et al., 2023). Collectively, these data demonstrate that acute exposure to extracellular α-syn aggregates is sufficient to drive border immune activation, lymphatic junctional remodelling, and size-selective impairment of CNS clearance.Although the α-syn PFF model recapitulates α-syn accumulation at the dura mater and other CNS border tissues, it represents an acute exposure paradigm, and the persistence of PFF-derived α-syn over time remains unclear. To model the sustained effects of extracellular α-syn on CNS border tissues, we generated an AAV9 vector encoding human α-syn fused to an SP2 secretion signal under the control of the CAG promoter (Prymaczok et al., 2024), enabling continuous secretion of α-syn into the CSF and surrounding border tissues. In vitro validation using mouse meningeal fibroblasts confirmed robust α-syn expression and secretion into the culture medium (Fig. 3, A–C). These results support a mechanism whereby sustained inflammation and pathological α-syn burden at CNS borders contribute to dysfunction of meningeal lymphatic clearance pathways in PD.Together, our data demonstrate that extracellular α-syn accumulation at CNS borders is not simply a downstream marker of PD pathology but can actively disrupt CNS clearance. We show that seeding-competent α-syn is present in both the CSF and dura mater of PD patients and that CSF exposure to α-syn in mice leads to accumulation around dural CAPs and lymphatic structures, uptake by border-associated phagocytes, innate immune activation, and impaired glymphatic and lymphatic clearance (Braak et al., 2003; Louveau et al., 2015; Malek et al., 2014; Mazzitelli et al., 2023; Rustenhoven et al., 2021; Smyth et al., 2024; Wiseman et al., 2025). Meningeal lymphatic dysfunction has previously been observed in PD patients and α-syn–based animal models, yet the mechanisms driving this impairment have remained unclear (Ding et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2019). Our findings suggest that extracellular α-syn within the CSF and dura can directly engage border immune and lymphatic compartments, positioning the dura mater as an active site of disease-relevant protein handling and immune regulation rather than a passive drainage interface.Our findings suggest that border-associated macrophages may represent a key cellular link between extracellular α-syn accumulation and impaired CNS clearance. In both our acute and chronic models, α-syn exposure was associated with dural macrophage activation, while chronic CSF-derived α-syn selectively elevated TNF without broad induction of other inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β or IFNγ. This aligns with meta-analyses reporting elevated TNF in the CSF and peripheral blood of patients with PD, supporting TNF as a disease-relevant inflammatory mediator across central and peripheral compartments (Qu et al., 2023). This is also consistent with previous work showing that α-syn can induce inflammatory activation states in border-associated macrophages and activate macrophages in CSF-draining LN (Liu et al., 2023; Schonhoff et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025). Together with our finding that TNF directly impairs LEC structure and function, these data suggest that macrophage-associated TNF is not merely a marker of inflammation, but a plausible mediator linking α-syn handling at CNS borders to lymphatic dysfunction (Kouchaki et al., 2018; Sriram et al., 2002). Although our data support border-associated macrophages as a likely source of TNF following α-syn exposure, we cannot exclude contributions from other dural immune populations, including T cells, mast cells, dendritic cells, or other myeloid populations (Lindestam Arlehamn et al., 2020; Mamuladze et al., 2025; Nakae et al., 2005; Rustenhoven et al., 2021; Serbina et al., 2003).There are also several important considerations. While our human data strongly support dural α-syn pathology as a disease-relevant feature of PD, larger postmortem cohorts will be required to determine its prevalence, temporal relationship to parenchymal pathology, and specificity across α-synucleinopathies. Future studies should also examine whether similar meningeal α-syn pathology occurs in Lewy body disease, multiple system atrophy, and cases with mixed neurodegenerative pathologies. Secondly, the acute PFF and chronic AAV-SP2-α-syn models used here were designed to isolate the effects of extracellular α-syn at CNS borders and do not fully reproduce the progressive neuronal origin and spread of α-syn pathology in PD. Importantly, these clearance and immune alterations occurred in the absence of overt brain parenchymal α-syn accumulation, gliosis, dopaminergic neuron loss, or behavioral impairment, suggesting that CNS border dysfunction can occur without detectable brain pathology. This raises the possibility that impaired lymphatic clearance and α-syn accumulation at CNS borders may form a feed-forward cycle, whereby extracellular α-syn disrupts drainage pathways and thereby further limits its own clearance. Thirdly, although our tracer drainage assays measure cumulative CSF efflux to cervical LNs and therefore capture drainage through multiple routes, our tissue analyses focused on dorsal and lateral dural regions associated with the dural sinuses. Basal meningeal lymphatics and nasopharyngeal lymphatic routes also contribute to CNS waste clearance and may therefore be similarly affected by CSF-derived α-syn (Chayama et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2025; Yoon et al., 2024). Future studies should determine whether α-syn–driven immune activation and lymphatic junctional remodelling also occur across these additional drainage pathways. However, the convergence of human dura pathology, mouse functional drainage deficits, macrophage-associated TNF production, and direct lymphatic endothelial disruption supports a model in which α-syn accumulation at CNS borders contributes to clearance failure in PD.These findings reveal CNS border tissues as actionable therapeutic sites in PD. Enhancing meningeal lymphatic drainage, improving CSF α-syn clearance, modulating border-associated macrophage activation, or limiting TNF-mediated lymphatic dysfunction may represent strategies to restore CNS waste clearance and interrupt the feed-forward cycle of α-syn accumulation, inflammation, and drainage failure (Alfaidi et al., 2025; Rustenhoven et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2023). Importantly, our data suggest that α-syn–driven lymphatic dysfunction is likely multifactorial, involving both direct effects of α-syn on LECs and indirect effects mediated through macrophage-associated TNF. Therefore, targeting TNF alone could attenuate inflammatory lymphatic dysfunction but may not fully restore clearance if α-syn aggregates continue to directly impair lymphatic endothelial integrity. At the end of the trial, mice were returned to their home cage and the arena was cleaned before the next animal was tested. Animals were trained over three consecutive days, with three trials per day. During each trial, the rod accelerated from 4 to 40 rpm over 5 min. A minimum inter-trial interval of 10 min was maintained to reduce fatigue.Following training, mice underwent a test session using the same accelerating protocol.
