TITLE: A multi-faceted hospital-based intervention for intrapartum care in sub-Saharan Africa: a stepped-wedge cluster-randomized trial

ABSTRACT:
AbstractAround three million babies are stillborn or die shortly after birth each year worldwide, underscoring a persistent and unacceptable burden on families and health systems. To help address this challenge, we developed the facility-based, multi-faceted Action Leveraging Evidence to reduce perinatal Mortality and Morbidity (ALERT) intervention. ALERT promoted essential practices of intrapartum childbirth using (i) co-design, (ii) training, (iii) quality improvement and (iv) leadership mentoring. We evaluated ALERT using a stepped-wedge cluster-randomized trial with five 6-month periods across 16 hospitals in Benin, Malawi, Tanzania and Uganda. Among 134,630 women (including 139,300 neonates), the intervention was associated with 22% lower odds of the composite co-primary outcome, early perinatal mortality, defined as fresh stillbirth and 24 h neonatal mortality (adjusted odds ratio 0.78, 95% confidence interval 0.65–0.94), but not with the co-primary outcome, fresh stillbirth (adjusted odds ratio 0.88, 95% confidence interval 0.68–1.13). Despite the limitation of only including 16 hospitals, the diversity of settings across four sub-Saharan African countries supports the finding that early perinatal mortality and morbidity could be reduced through implementation of multi-faceted, context-specific strategies to build maternity provider capacities. BODY:
MainHigh maternal and perinatal mortality and morbidity rates prevail in sub-Saharan African countries (SSA)1, 2–3. Nearly half of the global 1.9 million stillbirths and 2.3 million neonatal deaths annually occur in SSA, where both stillbirth and neonatal mortality rates remain above 20 per 1,000 births2. In addition, poorly managed labor and childbirth care leads to a large burden of morbidity, including cerebral palsy and neurological disorders caused by oxygen deprivation4, 5–6.Following the substantial increases in facility-based birth since the early 2000s, now exceeding 80% globally and over 60% in SSA today7,8, attention has shifted in recent years to the quality of pregnancy and childbirth care9,10. It is within this context that the World Health Organization (WHO) published a framework defining the quality of pregnancy and childbirth care in 201511 and recommendations for routine intrapartum care in 201812. This document complemented earlier guidance that focused on the management of the main maternal complications such as postpartum hemorrhage13, pre-eclampsia/eclampsia14, infection15 and preterm birth16. The later focus on the management of complications stimulated the development and evaluation of a large number of training programs17, 18–19. For example, the Preterm Birth Initiative focused on an intervention package addressing specifically prematurity-related intra- and immediate postnatal care, including administration of corticosteroids, resuscitation and kangaroo care20. The Nepal quality improvement package equally focused on resuscitation as an important component of immediate postpartum care21. In contrast, no training program operationalizing the WHO 2018 intrapartum care guideline has been developed, beyond trials assessing distinct tools, such as the Safe Childbirth Checklist22 or the new WHO partograph23. Intrapartum care, however, is complex and demands a multitude of skills and decisions within multi-professional maternity care teams including readiness to apply the packages to address complications. The lack of attention to routine care, however, hinders programming for the Every Woman Every Newborn Everywhere (EWENE) global initiative, midwifery care models as well as more efficient management when complications occur24.Guidelines and trainings are important steps towards improved care; however, achieving high-quality care requires a wider perspective. A variety of strategies have been proposed, spanning from certification and accreditation to quality assurance, collaborative quality improvement, and audit and feedback25, 26–27. Reviews on what works to improve healthcare providers’ adherence to evidence-based guidelines have indicated that combinations of strategies including quality improvement strategies to build problem-solving competences show the greatest effects28, 29–30. However, strategies with too many components are challenging to implement. The optimal combinations of implementation strategies addressing the complexity of intrapartum care considering implementability and scalability are yet to be defined. Furthermore, while co-design has been identified as an important strategy to shape contextually relevant solutions, the operationalization and value within quality improvement programs are unknown31.The Action Leveraging Evidence to reduce perinatal Mortality and Morbidity in sub-Saharan Africa (ALERT) project addresses these two knowledge gaps and developed, implemented and evaluated a multi-faceted intervention to improve intrapartum care through a stepped-wedge cluster-randomized trial in 16 hospitals across 4 sub-Saharan African countries: Benin, Malawi, Tanzania and Uganda. The intervention was composed of (i) end-user participation of women, families and midwifery providers to co-design the intervention, (ii) in-service midwifery competency-based training, (iii) quality improvement, and (iv) empowerment and leadership mentoring of maternity unit leaders32 (Fig. 1). ALERT focused on proven evidence-based clinical interventions as well as the experience of care to improve the quality of care in a holistic manner and adapted to context. The intervention started with a co-design process which informed six training modules. The 3-day training sessions were supported by Laerdal Global Health MamaBirthie low-cost models33. Quality improvement used the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles and promoted the documentation using quality improvement journals. Quarterly on-site mentoring and frequent phone calls with the maternity unit’s head supported the improvement work. The 14% increase in the odds of cesarean sections was within expected ranges, supporting the safety of the intervention.Post hoc analysisSensitivity, per-protocol analysis and temporal trendsWe performed a sensitivity analysis excluding three hospitals following discussions of preliminary results with country teams in December 2024, which identified three hospitals (Tanzania Hospitals 3 and 4 and Malawi Hospital 3) as outliers owing to implementation barriers including limited staff engagement and data quality concerns. The three-hospital exclusion was post hoc, triggered by pre-specified data-quality monitoring indicators combined with structured contextual review with country teams in December 2024 (criteria in Supplementary Table 4).DiscussionIn this stepped-wedge cluster-randomized trial in 16 high-caseload hospitals in Benin, Malawi, Tanzania and Uganda, we developed, implemented and evaluated a multi-faceted intervention of co-design, training, quality improvement and mentorship. The intervention was associated with 22% lower odds of the composite outcome in-facility early perinatal mortality, 19% lower odds of hypoxic–ischemic events and a 14% increase in cesarean sections. Mortality reductions were likely underpinned by improved processes such as increased fetal heart rate monitoring and cesarean sections. We did not observe any effects on responsiveness or respectful care.Our results suggesting 22% lower odds of in-facility early perinatal mortality concur with larger evaluations done in similar settings. The Preterm Birth Initiative in Kenya and Uganda reported 34% reduced odds of mortality in preterm neonates in response to a comprehensive quality improvement strategy20. Positive effects of interventions combining training, quality improvement and mentorship to improve intrapartum and early neonatal care are described by the Nepal Perinatal Quality Improvement study21,37,38, and the hospital initiative in the Democratic Republic of Congo39. Together, these findings reinforce the importance of multi-component strategies that address skills, systems and leadership simultaneously to achieve meaningful and sustained improvements in evidence-based practice and mortality, even if they are potentially resource intensive, as raised by others40. However, our economic analysis suggested limited costs of our approach41. For example, our co-design approach was not implemented in all hospitals and the mentoring approach relied largely on digital communication. In summary, our results support the importance of combining strategies to obtain greater changes in evidence-based practices and mortality28,42, while initiatives relying on quality improvement or training alone are increasingly recognized as insufficient to produce comparable mortality gains in resource-constrained settings20,43,44.Our results also highlight the potential of routine intrapartum care as opposed to addressing emergency obstetric care as previous training and improvement packages have done17, 18–19. Furthermore, our reduction in perinatal mortality and morbidity was achieved without technological innovations45. This highlights the potential effects of strengthening routine intrapartum care, that is, preventive and promotive midwifery care—an important finding towards the programming of the Every Woman Every Newborn Everywhere (EWENE) initiative24.Our intervention adopted a health system strengthening lens targeting recurrent, cross-cutting challenges in intrapartum care46. Through co-design, the ALERT team and maternity care providers jointly identified key quality-of-care deficits, their underlying causes and how these were shaped by local healthcare constraints including providers’ competences and attitudes47. For example, co-design interviews with women postpartum highlighted that women missed important information on labor progress and why decisions were made48. Furthermore, hierarchical structures and power imbalances between women and providers led to breakdowns in communications49. These findings led to developing specific training components on communication and teamwork. We believe the co-design process created an empowering working relationship and traction for change (M.S., manuscript in preparation). The competency-based training reinforced essential intrapartum practices and translated co-designed solutions into routine clinical behaviors36.We recognize that the positive effect on mortality was accompanied by an increase in cesarean section, which we interpret as a positive health systems strengthening effect. Population-based cesarean section rates in the included countries are at or below 10%, thus at levels where we would not expect an overuse of cesarean section50,51. Still, in low-resource hospital settings such as the ones in this study, cesarean section alone does not guarantee improved survival, particularly when delays in recognition or referral persist52,53. However, when considered alongside reductions in early perinatal mortality and hypoxic–ischemic events, the increase in cesarean section likely reflects more timely identification of complications and appropriate escalation of care. We therefore interpret this pattern as consistent with improved quality of intrapartum care rather than harmful overuse52.Our intervention showed no effect on responsiveness or on respectful care, although the teams worked intensively to improve responsiveness, including companionship. Several possible reasons may explain this. First, we cannot exclude the possibility that our tools were not sensitive enough to capture change. Several studies have indicated that exit interviews before discharge are likely to underreport abuse and mistreatment, and this could have led to the fact that we could not report an effect54,55. Second, interpersonal skills, attitudes and behaviors might need more time before sustained changes can be expected. Third, policy and structural constraints made it difficult to introduce companionship56. Together, these factors may explain the absence of measurable effects on responsiveness and respectful care within the trial period.The strength of this multi-country trial is the use of the stepped-wedge intervention design, a large number of births, and the inclusion of public and faith-based hospitals in four countries, improving generalizability. The use of the stepped-wedge trial design meant that all hospitals received the intervention, which enhanced engagement. Another strength was that while we adhered to structured processes, the outlined implementation strategy and a standardized curriculum, co-design, mentoring and quality improvement addressed local constraints in a flexible manner, thus arriving at a context-adapted, co-designed intervention with relevance to local context. We kickstarted our intervention using co-design methodologies, which allowed providers to influence the intervention and targeted routine intrapartum care, underscoring the potential of improving basic childbirth and preventive midwifery care as opposed to specifically addressing selected emergency obstetric conditions45,57.Our analytical approach had several strengths. First, we used hospital-specific mixed-effects models, rather than a single pooled analysis, acknowledging heterogeneity, confirmed by I2 values exceeding 50%. This avoided the ecological fallacy of assuming uniform intervention effects across settings. Second, the random-effects meta-analysis preserved this heterogeneity while providing overall estimates. Forest plots transparently displayed both individual hospital effects and uncertainty. Third, the intention-to-treat framework maintained the benefits of randomization, while sensitivity analyses (excluding sites with data quality concerns and per-protocol analysis) tested the robustness of our findings. Fourth, we had minimal missing data, reducing selection bias.Our study faces limitations, one of which was the start of our trial in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic, which precluded face-to-face meetings during the critical co-design and start-up period. The trial started with a 6-month delay, limiting the financial space. One consequence was that we were unable to introduce the lactate testing in a sub-sample.Our two primary and four of six secondary outcomes were assessed using an innovative e-registry data collection tool supported by rigorous quality assurance. Still, we cannot fully exclude underreporting of perinatal events, which is why we included a sensitivity analysis. National accountability measures, as applied in Tanzania, disincentivized maternity providers from reporting deaths for fear of repercussions58. Furthermore, we were not able to follow up neonates for longer than 24 h after childbirth, thus beyond discharge, to establish neonatal mortality. While we achieved extremely low missing data (<1% for primary outcomes), we acknowledge that in high-volume settings, emphasis on completeness could potentially affect accuracy. We note that the 4-month pre-intervention data collection phase supported the attained completeness rates of the primary and secondary outcomes59. However, we were unable to ensure the consistent documentation of some processes during labor, although planned. In consequence, we had over 90% missing data for the process measure on decision-to-birth time for cesarean section. Our data quality assurance focused on the primary and secondary outcomes.We used a composite indicator to account for misclassification between fresh stillbirth and very early neonatal deaths. Our study was not powered to indicate a 20%, but only a 30% reduction of the fresh stillbirth rate, which led to imprecise estimates. Similarly, other estimates had large CIs, pointing to the fact that the trial had limited power as adverse perinatal events are fortunately rare. We analyzed the cesarean section rate at two time points together with our data safety and monitoring board as a strong increase would have pointed to a negative effect, but had no other pre-defined stopping rules in view of the type of intervention. No alpha-spending procedures were used, and no trial decisions were based on interim statistical testing.The DerSimonian–Laird method, while standard, can underestimate uncertainty when combining results from relatively few clusters. We made no adjustment for multiple testing despite examining numerous secondary outcomes and process indicators, increasing the risk of Type I errors. The stepped-wedge design with its inherent temporal trends poses analytical challenges; while our models included time period as a fixed effect, residual confounding from unmeasured time-varying factors remains possible. Finally, hospital-level randomization, while pragmatic, limited our ability to adjust for potential imbalances in women-level characteristics that may have occurred despite randomization. We pre-specified only two primary outcomes and did not apply a formal multiplicity correction; secondary and process outcomes should therefore be interpreted on consistency of direction rather than on individual P values. Neonates delivered as multiples (5,409 women, 4.0% of deliveries) were treated as independent observations in the hospital-specific models; given the low multiple-birth prevalence and the consistent direction of effect shown in the pre-specified sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Tables 5–7), we expect any residual Type I error inflation from unmodelled within-mother clustering to be small.Our multi-centered and multi-country stepped-wedge, cluster-randomized trial suggests that the multi-faceted ALERT intervention reduced perinatal mortality and morbidity. The effects were strongest for early perinatal mortality, but consistent across the indicators of morbidity and mortality. Our economic analysis estimated incremental costs per perinatal death averted of US$805, US$2,651, US$3,188 and US$760 in Benin, Malawi, Tanzania and Uganda, respectively, based on post-implementation perinatal mortality rates, with travel costs representing the largest cost component41.The process evaluation confirmed the synergistic effects across the four intervention components. Co-design was reported to have an empowering effect, to enhance the relevance of the training, to reveal previously untapped resources and to improve coordination among the different actors involved in hospital quality improvement activities. Competency-based training was considered valuable both for refreshing clinical skills and for prompting reflection on team interaction and communication. Quality improvement and leadership mentoring supported the identification of local solutions to a broader range of barriers to implementing evidence-based interventions60. Taken together, these findings suggest that the intervention has potential for scalability. It was perceived as relevant and contextually aligned, demonstrated effectiveness and incurred limited costs61. Our approach to using co-design only in selected facilities and providing a mentoring follow-up using WhatsApp further increased feasibility and cost-effectiveness.In summary, the findings highlight the potential of strengthening routine intrapartum care, thus preventive and promotive midwifery care. Our model, including co-design and strengthening midwifery competences and leadership, may serve as a model to complement the ongoing large-scale work on neonatal care within EWENE. Our trial confirms the relevance of combining interventions with a synergistic effect.MethodsTrial design and oversightALERT was a stepped-wedge cluster-randomized trial with five 6-month periods, implemented by the ALERT Consortium in four hospitals in each of Benin, Malawi, Tanzania and Uganda32. The cluster (hospital) design was chosen as the intervention was delivered at the hospital level, targeting providers in the maternity ward. A stepped-wedge design was intended to mirror scale-up for policy buy-in and for statistical efficiency as we expected larger cluster-level differences62. ALERT is registered (17 June 2020) in the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry at 202006793783148. The first and second authors, the principal investigators and the trial statistician, respectively, attest to the accuracy and completeness of the data and the adherence to the study protocol. Furthermore, the ALERT intervention was developed using a co-design process with end-users including women, families and midwifery providers from each country.Roles and responsibilities were agreed upon among collaborators ahead of the research, and capacity-building plans for local researchers were implemented, including training of PhD students. As outlined in the Ethics statement, the ALERT study was approved by a local ethics review committee in each participating country. We have cited several local and regional research relevant to the ALERT study.Settings, participating hospitals and participantsThe four countries were purposively selected as they reflect different health system contexts and leveraged pre-existing research collaborations (Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Details on recruitment and setting are published elsewhere32,63,64. The intervention was implemented in 16 hospitals (3 public and 1 faith-based per country)32,47. Aspects of variations in midwifery education65 and knowledge and skills to provide quality intrapartum and neonatal care are presented elsewhere36. The hospitals faced human resource limitations and insufficient equipment and supplies.We included all women who gave birth to a live or stillborn infant >1,000 g between 1 July 2021 and 31 December 2023 in an intention-to-treat analysis66. We expanded the inclusion period in Benin, Malawi and Tanzania to February 2024 because of implementation delays in our per-protocol analysis (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Information 9, Statistical Analysis Plan). We excluded mother-baby pairs that arrived at the hospitals after giving birth, unless one of multiples was born in the participating hospital.InterventionThe ALERT intervention focused on the quality of intrapartum care targeting maternity providers in the included hospitals. We developed the intervention using a co-design process complemented by facility and knowledge assessments48,49,67 (January and September 2021) (Fig. 1). Training materials were developed addressing knowledge deficits and implementation challenges17,68. The implementation started with a 3-day training33, during which locally prioritized improvement topics were selected. Subsequently, maternity teams implemented and evaluated improvements under mentorship from ALERT researchers. The focus on mortality and experience of care was thought to be complementary. Responsiveness offers an opportunity to adapt care to changing women’s needs, promote women’s access to effective interventions and improve the quality of services34.OutcomesThe trial’s primary outcomes were in-facility (fresh) stillbirths defined as the number of fresh stillbirths of at least 1,000 g per 1,000 live- and stillbirths and a composite early perinatal mortality indicator defined as in-facility (fresh) stillbirths and 24 h neonatal mortality defined as death of a neonate within 24 h after birth per 1,000 live- and stillbirths, both assessed using our perinatal e-registry32. Secondary outcomes were hypoxic–ischemic events defined as a neonate with Apgar <7 at 5 min, neonatal seizures, cesarean sections, severe maternal morbidities defined as women with hysterectomy, laparotomy, blood transfusion, admission to an intensive care unit or referral to a higher-level facility, all expressed per 1,000 live- and stillbirths using the perinatal e-registry. Our respectful-care-focused secondary outcomes were responsiveness and respectful treatment (1 minus mistreatment). They were assessed using exit interviews with women after having given birth. Process measures were not pre-specified owing to the co-design of the intervention leading to adapted training and intervention components in the development phase. We report on fetal heart rate check at admission, fetal monitoring during the first stage of labor, detection of fetal distress, resuscitation of babies, companionship and breastfeeding within 1 h of birth. Details of the process indicators are listed in Supplementary Table 8. Cost-effectiveness and implementation process findings will be reported elsewhere35.Sample sizeWe used a sample size formula for stepped-wedge trials32,69. The study was sufficiently powered (75–80%) to detect a 25% reduction in the outcome perinatal mortality with baseline rates between 1.4% and 2.0% and a 30% reduction in intrapartum stillbirth assuming a baseline rate of 1% and 95% CIs. Details are available in the supplementary file of the protocol32.Data collectionData from patient files, birth registers and case notes were entered by the maternity unit heads and quality-improvement focal persons (who also received ALERT training and mentoring) in a preprogrammed e-registry application in the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform59. These staff underwent a 2-day training in March 2021, after which a 4-month pilot data collection phase started. Each tablet had a personalized token and password; data were uploaded daily. Data quality assurance included (1) automated data validation and logical checks in REDCap, (2) bi-weekly audits by running pre-prepared Stata do-files, and (3) cross-checking of entries against paper records.To assess experience of care, we conducted exit interviews using a questionnaire adapted from established responsiveness and mistreatment questionnaires70,71. We performed an exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis, which identified six factors: ‘Communication & supportive care’, ‘Hospital environment’, ‘Maintained respect & dignity’, ‘Social support’, ‘Maintained privacy & confidentiality’ and ‘Lack of physical & verbal abuse’. Findings were summarized in two summative scores to construct a responsiveness and respectful care (1 minus mistreatment) score72. In line with the stepped-wedge trial, we included a sample of 50 women per hospital recruited into the perinatal e-registry at five time periods. Data collectors applied a randomization factor to recruit the target number in each hospital based on the number of eligible women being discharged from the study hospitals73.Selection of hospitals and randomizationHospitals were purposively selected in March 2020, drawing from a national facility list to identify high-caseload hospitals. Criteria included (i) minimum caseload of 2,500 births per year; (ii) availability of cesarean section and blood transfusion; (iii) preferably located in rural districts; (iv) consisting of a mix of typical public and (faith-based) hospitals; and (v) interest in collaboration and no other ongoing initiative focusing on intrapartum care32. The final selection was done in consultation with the Ministry of Health and took into account feasibility concerns in terms of travel time between the hospitals to ensure operational efficiency.Randomization using Stata and a pre-prepared code was carried out on 17 June 2020 by an independent statistician after obtaining consent from the participating hospitals (Supplementary Information Statistical Analysis Plan). Stratification by country was used to enable random allocation of the four hospitals. Maternity providers were not blinded to the intervention, and women and their families receiving care at these hospitals were aware of the quality improvement intervention.Safety monitoringSafety oversight was provided by the independent Data Safety Monitoring Board. The preliminary Statistical Analysis Plan stated the development of clear stopping rules at approximately 50% and 75% of the planned sample size using pre-specified stopping criteria. However, in consultation with the Data Safety Monitoring Board, we agreed that clear stopping rules are difficult to apply for a non-clinical intervention such as training and quality improvement, which is universally perceived as valuable.We monitored the safety of our approach by analyzing and discussing changes in the cesarean section rates during meetings with our Data and Safety Monitoring Board. Our main outcomes were maternal and neonatal events constituting safety measures. In addition, we monitored, as part of our process evaluation, any severe events happening in the hospitals such as disruptions owing to climate or political events, staff rotation, interruptions in supplies, allegations owing to severe events in the maternity and similar. Our team followed a debriefing protocol where such events were discussed within the ALERT team to support the mentors in acting appropriately.Statistical analysisWe used an intention-to-treat approach, analyzing all data at woman- and baby-level within the 16 hospitals according to their assigned intervention status. The 30-month study period was divided into 5 discrete 6-month intervals (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Information 9, Statistical Analysis Plan).Descriptive analyses using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and median and interquartile ranges for continuous variables were conducted. Hospital-specific mixed-effects regression models were fitted to account for heterogeneity. We first considered generalized estimating equations adjusting for clusters and for the small sample32. However, in view of the heterogeneity, this statistical approach was not implementable.Two primary outcomes were pre-specified. No formal adjustment for multiplicity was planned or applied because both outcomes were considered clinically important and the analyses followed the pre-specified statistical analysis plan. The two outcomes are closely related, with fresh stillbirth forming part of the composite perinatal mortality outcome.For binary outcomes (primary and composite outcome) as well as the binary secondary outcomes, we used logistic mixed-effects models with random intercepts for intervention periods and presented ORs (95% CI). For continuous outcomes (responsiveness and respectful treatment scores), we used linear mixed-effects models and presented mean differences (95% CI). To account for time period effects and the stepped-wedge design structure, all models included the treatment status (intervention versus comparison) and time period (6-month intervals). Random intercepts were used for sequential randomization groups to account for clustering.Hospital-specific effects were subsequently pooled using random-effects meta-analysis with the DerSimonian–Laird method and displayed as forest plots. Heterogeneity was assessed using I2 statistics, with values above 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. We conducted sensitivity analyses by excluding hospitals identified as outliers based on both statistical and contextual considerations. Following discussions of preliminary results with country teams in December 2024, three hospitals (Tanzania Hospitals 3 and 4 and Malawi Hospital 3) were identified as outliers owing to fundamental implementation barriers, including lack of fetal monitoring capacity, data quality and limited staff engagement (Supplementary Table 4).Per-protocol analysisWe also conducted a per-protocol analysis using an alternative definition of the intervention period to ensure full implementation fidelity (Extended Data Fig. 3). Secondary outcomes and process indicators are presented without adjustment for multiple testing, as pre-specified in the Statistical Analysis Plan; they should be interpreted on consistency of direction and magnitude rather than on individual P values.Temporal trendsTemporal trends over time across all facilities were examined for primary and select secondary outcomes (Extended Data Fig. 4). As missing data for the primary outcomes were minimal (<1%), complete case analysis was done. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata Standard Edition 18.Sensitivity analysesIn response to peer review, three pre-specified sensitivity analyses were additionally performed (HKSJ pooling, per-protocol re-analysis and country-stratified random-effects pool); full methodological detail and results are provided in Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Tables 5–7.Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman poolingBecause the number of clusters is small (k = 15–16), we re-pooled the hospital-specific estimates using the HKSJ method, which combines a restricted-maximum-likelihood estimate of between-hospital variance with a t-distribution on k − 1 degrees of freedom. Point estimates were essentially unchanged from the DerSimonian–Laird pool used in the main analysis, and CIs were slightly wider (Supplementary Table 6).Per-protocol analysis considering quality concernsThree hospitals (Tanzania Hospitals 3 and 4 and Malawi Hospital 3) were prospectively identified as unable to deliver the intervention package, following country-team review meetings in December 2024 and before the analytical code was locked. Three criteria, agreed in advance, were applied: (i) documented absence of core intervention components (for example, Malawi Hospital 3 had no functional fetal monitoring equipment); (ii) implementation barriers recorded in site-level process-evaluation notes, such as low staff engagement and concerns about data fidelity at Tanzania Hospital 3 following an external audit, and low case volume with incomplete outcome data at Tanzania Hospital 4; and (iii) hospital-period cells in which the full package was not delivered, captured by the trial’s ‘perprotocol’ indicator. The hospitals meeting these criteria are listed in Supplementary Table 4. The pool was restricted to hospital-periods with ‘perprotocol = 1’. Point estimates attenuated for most outcomes, but their direction was unchanged; between-hospital heterogeneity (I2) fell substantially (Supplementary Table 7).Country-stratified poolingThe 16 hospital-specific estimates were pooled within each country, and a between-country Q-test with 3 degrees of freedom was used to test for heterogeneity of treatment effect across Benin, Malawi, Tanzania and Uganda. For the primary outcome of early perinatal mortality, there was no evidence of between-country heterogeneity (Qᵇ(3) = 1.61, P = 0.66). For fresh stillbirth, cesarean section, severe maternal morbidity, fetal distress and breastfeeding within 1 h, between-country heterogeneity was significant (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 7).Protocol deviationsWe were not able to implement lactate testing as a measure independent of clinical judgment to indicate hypoxic–ischemic events for operational and financial reasons. However, we included lactate testing using the Nova lactate point-of-care meter for 437 cases of Apgar <7 in 2 hospitals in Uganda. A total of 420 of 437 (96%) neonates born with an Apgar <7 had a lactate level above 5.5 mmol l−1, confirming earlier work indicating reasonable sensitivity and specificity of the Apgar score in this setting52,74.
