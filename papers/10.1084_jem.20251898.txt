TITLE: Dysregulated AKT signaling reprograms osteosarcoma to drive selective reliance on EP300

ABSTRACT:
Here, Delahunty et al. identify that EP300 controls the growth of AKT-dysregulated osteosarcoma cells by regulating expression of state-specific genetic dependencies. AKT inhibitors synergize with EP300 degraders to suppress osteosarcoma growth in vitro and in vivo. These agents positively combine across cancer cells in a manner dependent on control of protein synthesis.

BODY:
IntroductionGene expression is controlled by interactions of epigenetic enzymes and transcription factors, which establish and maintain gene promoter, enhancer, repressor, and insulator elements (Kwak et al., 2013; Margueron and Reinberg, 2010; Tie et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2009; Whyte et al., 2013). Driven by the activity of epigenetic enzymes, promoter and enhancer elements are either activated or repressed, leading to alterations in gene expression and the resultant malignant phenotype (Dawson and Kouzarides, 2012; Dawson et al., 2012; Jenuwein and Allis, 2001; Li and Seto, 2016). In cancer cells, these enzymes may be co-opted, driving aberrant activation or repression of gene expression and resulting in malignant phenotypes such as uncontrolled proliferation, repressed differentiation, or migratory behaviors (Wimalasena et al., 2020). How the activity of these enzymes is shaped by mutated or activated oncogenes to result in dysregulated transcription is an area of active interest. Further, since cancer cells broadly rely on epigenetic enzymes to control the malignant transcriptional program, and these enzymes may contain catalytic or non-catalytic binding pockets suitable for targeting with small molecules, there has been extensive interest in pharmacologic development and targeting of these proteins (Liu et al., 2024; Wimalasena et al., 2020; Zhou and Cole, 2025).Two central proteins involved in the control of gene expression are EP300 and CBP (Arany et al., 1994; Ogryzko et al., 1996). EP300 and CBP (KAT3B and KAT3A) are paralogous lysine acetyltransferases that establish acetylation marks across the proteome, including acetylation of histone H3, lysine-27 (H3K27ac), a mark of active enhancer and promoter elements (Creyghton et al., 2010; Weinert et al., 2018; Zhou and Cole, 2025). These proteins are commonly co-expressed in cancer cells (Durbin et al., 2022); however, some cancer cells display enhanced reliance on one of these proteins due to mechanisms including selective recruitment by lineage-specific transcription factors (Durbin et al., 2022) or mutations in the other (Ogiwara et al., 2016). Due to their key role in promoting malignancy, medicinal chemistry efforts have led to small-molecule histone acetyltransferase (HAT), bromodomain (BRD), or KIX-domain inhibitors that target both of these proteins (Joy et al., 2021; Lasko et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2024; Ramaswamy et al., 2018; Shendy et al., 2024; Welti et al., 2021; Zhou and Cole, 2025). However, the high amino acid sequence homology between EP300 and CBP in these targetable domains hinders selective targeting, since available small-molecule inhibitors targeting EP300 typically also target CBP. These properties may lead to undesirable toxicities in untransformed cells, thereby reducing a potential therapeutic window. To take advantage of the enhanced reliance of individual tumor types on either EP300 or CBP, recently, we and others reported first-generation proteolysis-targeted chimeras (PROTACs), including JQAD1 and others, that selectively target EP300 for proteasomal degradation, with minimal effects on CBP (Chen et al., 2024; Durbin et al., 2022; Marsh et al., 2025). These compounds are well tolerated in vivo, likely due to the retention of CBP partially compensating for the loss of EP300 in untransformed cells (Durbin et al., 2022). These PROTACs, which drive proximity-based ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of EP300 by bridging to the E3 ubiquitin ligase receptor cereblon (CRBN), are dependent on expression of CRBN (Durbin et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023). However, key additional questions remain, including what mechanisms control compound efficacy and which tumor lineages may be optimal to selectively target EP300 or CBP.Thus, here, we integrate functional genomics and chemical biology analyses to identify cell lines representing cancer lineages with enhanced reliance on EP300, compared with CBP, that are also more sensitive to the EP300-targeted PROTAC, JQAD1. We identify that EP300 is generally more required than CBP for growth of cancer cells across 53 cancer lineages. Osteosarcoma (OS), an aggressive bone sarcoma with no available targeted agents, is sensitive to genetic loss of EP300. A genetic subtype of OS, marked by dysregulation of the AKT signaling pathway, is specifically sensitive to JQAD1. We demonstrate that ectopic AKT activity drives sensitivity to JQAD1 in OS cells by enforcing genome-wide relocalization of EP300, CBP, and H3K27ac to drive the expression of genes required for the growth of AKT-dysregulated OS. To enhance the effects of single-compound targeting with JQAD1, we demonstrate that combinations of AKT inhibitors and JQAD1 synergistically suppress the growth of AKT-dysregulated OS cells in vitro and in orthotopic murine models in vivo. Testing this model across >850 cancer cell lines, we observe frequent additive to synergistic interactions between AKT inhibitors and JQAD1, related to AKT inhibitor–mediated disruption of protein synthesis. These observations provide a foundation for consideration of combination therapies targeting EP300 and AKT across multiple cancer lineages marked by elevated AKT signaling and EP300 dependency and identify a genetically defined subtype of OS with exceptional responses to combination treatment.ResultsEP300 is an enriched dependency and selectively required in OSTargeting one of a paralogous, co-expressed set of proteins may enhance a therapeutic window by sparing the other paralog in untransformed cells. This strategy is challenging using small-molecule inhibitors with nonspecific activity against closely related paralogs. Previously, we developed the small-molecule PROTAC degrader of EP300, JQAD1, and demonstrated it had single-agent efficacy in pediatric neuroblastoma (Durbin et al., 2022). JQAD1 is well tolerated in vivo with broad anticancer effects across different cancer cell lines (Durbin et al., 2022). Genetic disruption of either EP300 or CBP caused loss of fitness in 45 cell lines (3%) (Fig. 1 A). Organizing cells by tumor cell lineage and ranking by the ratio of genetic dependency on EP300 versus CBP, we found that most lineages displayed enhanced genetic dependency on EP300, compared with CBP. Few lineages, such as anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, mesothelioma, cervical squamous cell carcinoma, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma, displayed enhanced reliance on CBP compared with EP300 (Fig. 1 B). As previously (Durbin et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023), neuroblastoma showed enhanced reliance on EP300 compared with CBP. However, other lineages displayed even greater relative dependency on EP300 (Fig. 1 B). To identify lineages potentially susceptible to pharmacologic disruption using the EP300-targeted protein degrader, JQAD1, we reanalyzed our prior screening data, in which JQAD1 was tested across 544 cancer cell lines (Durbin et al., 2022). Of the top six lineages nominated by DepMap analysis, JQAD1 frequently reduced cell growth in OS cells, comparably with neuroblastoma (Fig. 1 C and Tables S1 and S2). While only 7/27 (25.9%) of OS cell lines were genetically dependent on EP300 using a stringent CHRONOS Score cutoff of < −0.5, none were genetically dependent on CBP (Fig. 1 D). In comparison, while 20/42 (47.6%) of neuroblastoma cell lines were genetically dependent on EP300, these cell lines were also occasionally dependent on CBP (2/41 = 4.9%) (Fig. 1 D). Similarly, in the CCMA dataset, EP300 was a genetic dependency in 4/9 OS cell lines, and no cells were dependent on CBP (Fig. S1, C and D). Thus, a proportion of OS cell lines are both highly dependent on EP300 but not CBP and are susceptible to JQAD1 treatment.OS is a genetically disordered tumor with rapid tumor evolution and unstable genetics (Espejo Valle-Inclan et al., 2025; Meltzer and Helman, 2021). EP300 is implicated in OS, functioning to reorganize enhancer elements that drive pro-metastatic gene expression (Morrow et al., 2018). OS genomes often bear mutations in TP53 and RB1, amplifications of c-MYC, CDK4, CCNE1, and AKT1, and loss of PTEN and ATRX (Chen et al., 2014; Kovac et al., 2015; Sayles et al., 2019). These genetic lesions may have prognostic significance; for example, loss of PTEN and amplification of c-MYC have each been associated with metastatic disease and poorer overall survival (Feng et al., 2020; Gong et al., 2017; Marinoff et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2019). Contributing to the dismal survival rates in metastatic/relapsed OS patients are limited advancements in targeted drug development. Thus, we next sought to determine if there were correlations between dependency on common genetic aberrations in OS and EP300 dependency. By correlating genomic events found in OS in the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) with DepMap dependency data (n = 27 cell lines), we did not observe any single-gene mutational patterns predisposing to EP300 dependency (Fig. 1 E). We did, however, observe that several EP300-dependent lines bore mutations in critical multi-gene pathways. In addition to deletion of PTEN, OS cell lines, as well as primary tumors and patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) (Perry et al., 2014; Sayles et al., 2019; Schott et al., 2024), often display copy number gain of upstream growth factors and receptors such as VEGFA, KDR, PDGFRA, and KIT, in addition to gains of PIK3CA, AKT1, or RICTOR and loss of PTEN. Since these mutations are components of a classical receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)–PI3K–AKT–mTOR pathway (Vasan and Cantley, 2022), we termed tumors bearing these mutations “AKT-dysregulated” OS.Next, we validated the effects of EP300 degradation in OS cell lines. In addition to conventional OS cell lines, to limit the potential confounding effects of genomically unstable cell lines in culture, we used a recently reported group of low-passage OS cell lines derived from PDXs (Sayles et al., 2019; Schott et al., 2024). JQAD1 elicited dose-dependent growth inhibition in four cell lines: one conventional (OS252) and three low-passage PDX cell lines: OS052, OS525, and OS384 (Fig. 1 F). Each of these harbored mutations consistent with AKT dysregulation, including multiple RTK copy number gains (OS384, OS252), RICTOR gains and PTEN loss (OS052), and amplification of AKT1 (OS525) (Fig. 1 G). In contrast, JQAD1-resistant cells (OS152, OS186, OS526, OS742, 143B, CAL72, U2OS, and SJSA1) harbored other genomic events, including amplification of c-MYC or CCNE1 (Fig. 1 G).To confirm that these genetic lesions resulted in dysregulated AKT activity, we performed western blotting of PDX-derived cells for activated AKT (phosphorylated Ser-473), in addition to total AKT and PTEN proteins. PhosphoSer-473-AKT was elevated in cell lines with predicted AKT dysregulation (Fig. S1 F). Next, we examined tissue sections from 27 human low-passage OS PDXs with matched whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data. WGS analysis of known AKT pathway members, along with c-MYC/MYCN/MYCL and other genes commonly mutated in OS, identified samples with isolated AKT pathway mutations (AKT dysregulated, n = 6/27), c-MYC/MYCN/MYCL gene amplifications (“MYC amplified,” n = 4/27), or no mutations in these genes (n = 17/27) (Table S3 and Fig. S1, G and H). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) demonstrated statistically significantly increased phospho-Ser473-AKT in predicted AKT-dysregulated PDXs, compared with other groups (Fig. 1, H–K; and Fig. S1, G and H). To identify if similar changes were also found in human samples, we examined sequencing panel-based data from 331 patients with OS, derived from the AACR GENIE database version 19.0 public cohort (AACR Project GENIE Consortium, 2017). AKT dysregulation mutations were found in 88/331 patients, which was similar to the proportion identified in the PDX models (Fig. 1 L and Fig. S1 I). These data suggest that mutations predicted to promote activation of the AKT pathway (“AKT dysregulation”) are associated with activation of the pathway in cell lines and PDXs in vivo and similarly found in human primary tumors. To examine this correlation, we focused on two JQAD1-sensitive, AKT-dysregulated OS cell lines, OS052 (PTEN-deleted) and OS525 (AKT1-amplified). Consistent with growth inhibition driven by JQAD1 (Fig. 1 F), we observed that JQAD1 caused profound dose-dependent suppression of colony formation (Fig. 2 A). This was not associated with changes in cell cycle measurements (Fig. S1 I) nor with induction of osteoblastic differentiation when adjusting for loss of cell number (Fig. S1 J). Next, we treated these cells with a range of doses of JQAD1 for 48 h, and performed western blotting to assess changes in protein expression. JQAD1 caused dose-responsive degradation of EP300, without effects on CBP, in both cell lines (Fig. 2 B). Consistent with a growth slowing/induction of apoptosis phenotype, we observed that JQAD1 caused increased expression of the marker of apoptosis, cleaved PARP1 (Fig. 2 B). Despite these cell lines not having genomic c-MYC amplifications, all tested OS cell lines are dependent on c-MYC (Fig. S2, A and B). So, we also performed western blotting of c-MYC proteins, which demonstrated that loss of EP300 correlated with loss of c-MYC. To understand if c-MYC proteins were reduced by loss of EP300 or due to apoptotic induction, we treated OS525 cells with JQAD1 in a time course. JQAD1 caused loss of EP300 and c-MYC proteins ∼24 h prior to activation of apoptosis, measured by cleaved PARP1 (Fig. 2 C), indicating that loss of c-MYC is temporally separated from apoptosis. As a control, we treated two c-MYC–amplified, JQAD1-resistant cell lines, OS152 and OS186, with JQAD1, which failed to demonstrate effects on EP300 or CBP (Fig. 2 D). To confirm that these cells remained sensitive to genetic loss of EP300 as predicted by DepMap analyses, we established Cas9-expressing OS052, OS525, and OS186 cells and infected these with sgRNAs targeting EP300 (EP300-1, EP300-2), CBP (CBP-1, CBP-2), no expressed gene (LACZ), or a gene desert (ch2.2). Western blotting confirmed loss of the relevant target protein (Fig. S2 C). In colony formation growth assays, as predicted, we observed that loss of EP300, but not CBP, resulted in loss of cell growth in all three cell lines (Fig. S2 D). To examine this hypothesis, we used c-MYC–amplified, JQAD1-resistant OS152 and OS186 cells. We identified the top 100 most synergistic and bottom 100 least synergistic (i.e., most antagonistic) cell lines and extracted their whole transcriptomic RNA-seq–based expression profiles from the CCLE (Fig. 4 D and Fig. S4 K, red-marked data points). GSEA showed that genes in the Hallmark PI3K–AKT–mTOR_Signaling and mTORC1_Signaling gene sets were upregulated in the most synergistic cell lines (Fig. 4 E). Thus, cells demonstrating JQAD1+AKT inhibitor synergy displayed transcriptional signatures suggestive of elevated protein synthesis.Low protein synthesis rates correlate with degrader sensitivityOne model for PROTAC function implies competition between protein synthesis and degradation rates (Mares et al., 2020) (Fig. 4 G). By this model, the PROTAC effect is enhanced in cells with reduced protein synthesis rates due to a failure to resynthesize protein species that have been depleted by proteasomal degradation. Since cells displaying synergy between MK-2206 and JQAD1 have transcriptional signatures of elevated protein synthesis, we hypothesized that AKT inhibitor-mediated suppression of protein synthesis may be a mechanism of potentiation of JQAD1 activity. To test this, we examined nascent protein synthesis rates in JQAD1-sensitive and insensitive cell lines using puromycin labeling assays. Western blotting of lysates prepared from OS cells pulsed with puromycin showed increased labeling in JQAD1-insensitive OS152 and OS186 cells, compared with OS052 and OS525 JQAD1-sensitive cells, suggesting a slower rate of nascent protein synthesis in JQAD1-sensitive cells (Fig. 4 H).Next, we tested the ability of AKT inhibition to potentiate the JQAD1 effect on EP300 by treating JQAD1-sensitive OS052 cells with a range of doses of JQAD1, along with either DMSO or MK-2206 at 1 μM. High-dose JQAD1 depleted EP300, resulting in loss of c-MYC proteins. However, when co-incubated with MK-2206, JQAD1 reduced both EP300 and c-MYC proteins at lower doses (Fig. 4, I and J). AKT control of protein synthesis is mediated via mTORC1 through distinct pathways, including phosphorylation-dependent activation of p70S6K or repression of 4EBP1 (He et al., 2025). These observations are specifically observed in AKT-dysregulated OS PDX cells in vitro and in vivo.DiscussionDespite their high sequence homology in functional domains, the paralogous HATs EP300 and CBP have distinct roles in the control of gene expression in a cell type–specific manner (Kasper et al., 2006; Kung et al., 2000; Ramos et al., 2010; Rebel et al., 2002; Shendy et al., 2024). These observations are not evaluable using available small-molecule inhibitors, since these are nonspecific between EP300 and CBP (Zhou and Cole, 2025). Despite this dual specificity for EP300 and CBP, small-molecule inhibition of EP300 and CBP has been extremely informative, yielding a higher layer of understanding of the functions of distinct domains of these proteins, which, when inhibited, can elicit disparate cellular phenotypes (Shendy et al., 2024; Zucconi et al., 2019). These tools have been useful to elucidate disparate roles of BRD-based scaffolding and catalytic activities of EP300 and CBP (Narita et al., 2021; Weinert et al., 2018). With the development of selective and nonselective degraders of EP300 (Chen et al., 2024; Durbin et al., 2022; Marsh et al., 2025; Vannam et al., 2021), temporally based and specific control of either EP300 or CBP is possible. These studies have demonstrated prominent and cell type–specific effects on control of oncogenic transcription by EP300 in specific contexts, as compared with CBP (Durbin et al., 2022). Importantly, EP300 degradation causes loss of both its catalytic activity that is required for assembly of the transcriptional preinitiation complex and pause release (Narita et al., 2021), and BRD-based scaffolding activities, required for docking of EP300 to acetylated proteins (Welti et al., 2021), among others. The effects of degradation are, in some cases, greater than small-molecule inhibition (Durbin et al., 2022; Mares et al., 2020), and with the clinical advancement of targeted protein degradation agents (Chirnomas et al., 2023), these observations make JQAD1 and other molecules like it with attractive toxicity profiles an intriguing modality for investigation, especially in high-risk, aggressive cancers.Here, we demonstrate that cell lines representing specific cancer subtypes display enhanced genetic dependency on EP300, as compared with CBP, and vice versa. These data reflect prior data demonstrating synthetic lethality on EP300 in CBP-mutant cancers (Ogiwara et al., 2016), though mutation in EP300 or CBP is not required for these effects, as in high-risk neuroblastoma cell lines which are wild-type for EP300 and CBP, and yet display selective dependency on EP300 (Durbin et al., 2022). Here, we extend these observations to cell lines representing multiple other cancer types, uncovering a predictable range of tumor models with enhanced dependency on EP300 or CBP for study. We identified cell lines from OS as a subtype that is highly sensitive to EP300 genetic knockout. Coupled with cell line degrader testing, we identified PDX-derived OS cell lines with enhanced dependency on EP300, linked by common dysregulation of the AKT signaling axis. OS is a challenging-to-manage bone tumor with no currently approved targeted therapies (Marina et al., 2016; Meltzer and Helman, 2021). Patients with metastatic OS have exceptionally poor survival (Meltzer and Helman, 2021) and are often characterized by either amplifications of c-MYC or dysregulation of AKT signaling, driven by loss of PTEN, mutation of PIK3CA, or amplification of upstream growth factor receptors or AKT1-3 (Feng et al., 2020; Gong et al., 2017; Marinoff et al., 2023; Perry et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2019). This selective efficacy of the EP300 degrader JQAD1 in suppressing the growth of AKT-dysregulated OS cells mechanistically requires AKT-driven relocalization of EP300 and CBP, resulting in rewiring of transcription to drive genetic dependency expression.Our studies provide two new key insights. First, AKT-dysregulated OS respond to EP300 degradation in vitro and in vivo, but non-AKT–dysregulated cell lines do not. These data provide a foundation for exploring the role of EP300 degraders in AKT–dysregulated OS, which is both genetically defined and high-risk, and represents up to an estimated 25% of OS tumors (Fig. 1 L) (Gong et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2019). Potential caveats to this are our observation of variable sizes of JQAD1-only treated tumors in vivo, though these findings were not statistically significant. We observed three mechanisms of AKT dysregulation: (1) amplification of AKT genes; (2) deletions of PTEN; and (3) co-amplifications of PDGFRA, KIT, and KDR with or without amplification of VEGFA. Whether each is similarly responsive to JQAD1 is an area of active exploration. Second, combinations of the AKT inhibitor MK-2206 with JQAD1 are synergistic in AKT-dysregulated but not in c-MYC–amplified, OS cells. These agents demonstrate positive interactions widely across cell lines from different cancer types, connected by their dysregulation of AKT pathways. Many tumors display mutational activation of the AKT axis, commonly through hotspot mutations in PIK3CA, deletion or mutations of PTEN, or mutation/amplification of AKT genes (Millis et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2017). While some OS PDX cell lines and tumors display genetic evidence of both c-MYC amplification and AKT dysregulation (Fig. 1, E, G, and L; and Fig. S1 L), in general, there is limited overlap between c-MYC gene amplifications or activation of the c-MYC proximal network and AKT pathway dysregulation across cancers (Schaub et al., 2018). These data point to important differences and additional layers of complexity when considering the role of these mutations in context-specific tumors, such as OS, compared with landscapes of tumor genetics. Despite these differences, these data indicate that AKT dysregulation may be a state in which combinations of AKT inhibitors and EP300 degraders demonstrate positive interactions. These data echo prior analyses in neuroblastoma demonstrating that genetic loss of PIK3CA enhances the effects of JQAD1 (Lee et al., 2023) and further draw distinctions between c-MYC–associated and AKT-driven pathways, both of which are associated with enhanced OS tumor growth and poor patient survival (Feng et al., 2020; Gong et al., 2017; Marinoff et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2019). With the recent clinical approval of AKT inhibitors (Dilawari et al., 2024), these observations may suggest a more general strategy to enhance the effects of either EP300 degraders or AKT inhibitors in specific genetic contexts.The activity of JQAD1 is dependent on expression of the E3 ubiquitin ligase receptor CRBN (Durbin et al., 2022). Here, we confirm these results, but now extend these to identify a critical role, across cancer cell lines, of protein synthesis in the efficiency of JQAD1. This goes to explain the prior observation of PIK3CA knockout causing sensitization to JQAD1 in neuroblastoma cell lines (Lee et al., 2023), but now extends this finding across cells from a myriad number of cancer types and suggests a potentially generalizable mechanism for enhancing the activity of PROTACs by slowing protein resynthesis rates. These data also highlight a seeming paradox, however: in OS cells, AKT-dysregulated activity is required for JQAD1 function, while across a variety of other cancer cell lines, suppression of AKT using inhibitors positively interacts with JQAD1. These findings may be partially harmonized by our observation of relatively reduced protein synthetic rates in AKT-dysregulated OS cells, compared with c-MYC–amplified cells. AKT is a central signaling node in human cancer cells that drives a myriad of pathways downstream (Vasan and Cantley, 2022), including direct phosphorylation of EP300 to promote transcriptional coactivation (Huang and Chen, 2005). Our analysis implicates roles for AKT-directed EP300, CBP, and H3K27ac localization, in addition to protein synthesis downstream of AKT, as being critical for these interactions. Whether additional pathways downstream of AKT are implicated in these responses represents the target of ongoing work in OS cells.In summary, we identify that OS models harboring dysregulation of the AKT pathway are genetically and pharmacologically sensitive to loss of the EP300 HAT, mediated by AKT-driven relocalization of EP300 proteins to drive the transcription of genetic dependencies in these cells. These findings suggest that AKT signaling promotes a downstream transcriptional program that may be shaped by epigenetic enzymes to drive genes responsible for lineage-specific cancer cell growth. We extend these observations to demonstrate synergy between AKT inhibitors and EP300 degraders across human cancer cell lines, identifying that transcriptional signatures of elevated protein translation are associated with enhanced combinatorial effects. The Bliss model (Bliss) assumes the two drugs act independently, with the model expectation beingVBLISS=Va×Vb.The highest single agent model (HSA) (Mathews Griner et al., 2014) follows the expectation that the combination viability is the smallest of the two single agents:VHSA=min Va,Vb.Because measured viabilities are capped at 1, the Bliss expectation is always less than or equal to the HSA expectation. The synergy score is computed as the difference between the model expectation and the observed viability—a positive value indicates synergy while a negative value indicates antagonism. For robustness, we combine both model expectations into a single synergy score, which effectively considers the lower viability of the two model expectations when looking for synergy and the higher viability of the two model expectations when looking for antagonism:S={VHSA−Vab,if VHSA<Vab0,if VBLISS≤Vab≤VHSAVBLISS−Vab,if Vab<VBLISS.This synergy score, averaged across combination doses, is the MSS used in analyses.
