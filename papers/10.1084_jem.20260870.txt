TITLE: Helios is critical for Treg effector differentiation in adipose autoimmunity

ABSTRACT:
Thornton et al. demonstrate that in Tregs, the transcription factor Helios directly controls Tcf1, Lef1, Id3, and Bach2 to regulate effector Treg generation and terminal differentiation. Mice with Helios-deficient Tregs generate dysfunctional effector Tregs resulting in a unique adipose-specific autoimmunity.

BODY:
IntroductionCD4+Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) suppress immune activation in a dominant manner and play a critical role in the maintenance of immune system homeostasis and self-tolerance (Rudensky, 2011; Shevach, 2011). Defects in Treg numbers, function, and stability have been proposed as major causative factors in both organ-specific and systemic models of autoimmunity. Paradoxically, many models of autoimmune disease are characterized by an increased frequency of Tregs, which express an “activated” phenotype, presumably, in an attempt to compensate for their dysfunction.Tregs also control tissue homeostasis, and adipose tissue (AT) represents one of the best studied examples of this Treg function (Becker et al., 2017; Burzyn et al., 2013; Castoldi et al., 2015; Panduro et al., 2016). The percentage of Tregs of total CD4+ T cells is higher in AT compared with lymphoid tissues, and their transcriptome is distinct compared with splenic Tregs (Feuerer et al., 2009; Winer et al., 2009). In particular, AT Tregs are more highly activated, are clonally restricted, and secrete high levels of IL-10 (Feuerer et al., 2009; Han et al., 2014). Mice fed a high-fat diet have fewer AT Tregs, but treatment with anti-IL-2/IL-2R complexes to increase the number of Tregs resulted in lower blood glucose and increased insulin tolerance (Feuerer et al., 2009). In addition, leptin-deficient obese (Ob/ob) mice also have fewer Tregs but when administered anti-CD3 and β-glucosylceramide, exhibited increased Tregs with a concurrent decrease in macrophages and blood glucose levels (Ilan et al., 2010). Importantly, AT Tregs express the adipose-specific transcription factor (TF), PPARγ, required for their maintenance and identity in AT (Cipolletta et al., 2012), and they express the IL-33R (ST2) (Han et al., 2015; Kolodin et al., 2015; Vasanthakumar et al., 2015). The importance of the IL-33/ST2 axis in AT homeostasis has been demonstrated in many studies. Ob/ob mice treated with IL-33 have an increase in Th2 cells, a repolarization of M1 to M2 (CD206+) macrophages, and improved insulin sensitivity (Miller et al., 2010). High-fat diet mice treated with IL-33 exhibit increased Tregs, and increased ST2 on Tregs, with a concurrent decrease in macrophages, blood glucose, and insulin resistance (Brestoff et al., 2015; Han et al., 2015), while global deletion of either ST2 or IL-33 led to a marked decrease of Tregs in AT and increased insulin resistance (Molofsky et al., 2015; Vasanthakumar et al., 2015).However, the intrinsic role of IL-33 signaling in Tregs has been questioned, as earlier studies were carried out in mice with germline deletions. Subsequently, one study in mice with a Treg-specific deletion of ST2 demonstrated that Tregs were, in fact, reduced in AT (Li et al., 2018), but Hemmers et al. did not note any differences in either number or phenotype of Tregs in the AT of mice with a Treg-specific deletion of ST2, calling into question the intrinsic role of IL-33 signaling in Treg maintenance in the adipose and highlighting the complex role that the ST2/IL-33 axis plays in adipose homeostasis (Hemmers et al., 2021).Recent studies have demonstrated that Tregs are a heterogeneous population and can be divided into three major subpopulations: resting Tregs (rTregs), activated Tregs (aTregs), and effector Tregs (eTregs). rTregs are characterized as CD62+Tcf1+, aTregs as CD62−Tcf1+, and eTregs as CD62−TCF− (Yang et al., 2019). eTregs may additionally express markers of terminal differentiation such as Klrg1, IRF4, and Batf (Sumida et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2019) and, analogous to effector CD4+ T cell subsets, can also be divided into T follicular regulatory cells (TFR), Th1-like, Th2-like, and Th17-like subsets, expressing the effector-specific TFs Bcl6, t-bet, Gata3, and RORγt, respectively. These eTreg subsets are present in peripheral lymphoid organs but are much more prevalent in tissue. In AT, two major eTreg subpopulations that have been described by Torres et al. are characterized by ST2 and CXCR3, respectively (Torres et al., 2024). In addition, Muñoz-Rojas et al. have used single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to identify seven AT Treg subpopulations including an ST+ population, as well as t-bet+, 4-1BB+, IL-18R1+, and quiescent cells (Muñoz-Rojas et al., 2024). As differentiation to eTregs must be tightly controlled to avoid nonspecific immunosuppression, the TF Id3 blocks aTreg progression to eTregs and is critical for maintaining aTreg and rTreg homeostasis (Sullivan et al., 2019). In addition, the TFs Tcf1, Lef1, and Bach2 also block differentiation to effector subsets but promote TFR differentiation (Sumida et al., 2024).Treg-specific deletion of the TF, Helios, in mice results in generalized T cell activation, T follicular helper (TFH) expansion, and a systemic Th1 profile (Sebastian et al., 2016). We show here that in contrast to Scurfy mice or mice with a deletion of Foxp3 which develop inflammation in multiple organs, organ-specific inflammation in Helios knockout (KO) mice is uniquely limited to AT. In marked contrast to other models of Treg dysfunction in the AT that exhibit metabolic disruption, Helios-deficient mice develop acquired lipodystrophy with extensive T cell infiltrate and destruction of AT, leading to hepatic steatosis, markedly enlarged livers, and insulin resistance. Importantly, lipodystrophy is exclusively mediated by autoreactive CD8+ T cells that destroy AT through Fas-L– and cytotoxic-mediated mechanisms. Phenotypic analysis of the AT Tregs in these Helios-deficient mice demonstrates that they are skewed to the eTreg phenotype (CD62Llo and Tcf1−) but fail to express markers of terminally differentiated eTregs such as CXCR3, ST2, and Klrg1. Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) studies demonstrate that Helios binds to sites in the Id3, Tcf7, Lef1, and Bach2 loci. As these genes collectively restrain eTreg development, our studies show that in the absence of Helios, the process of eTreg differentiation proceeds unchecked, resulting in enhanced but dysfunctional eTreg production. Thus, Helios is required both to maintain the aTreg pool and to generate proper terminally differentiated eTregs.ResultsMice with Helios-deficient Tregs develop CD8+ T cell–mediated acquired lipodystrophyWe have previously published that mice with a Treg-specific deletion of the TF Helios (Ikzf2fl/fl x Foxp3-Cre mice, hereafter KO) develop systemic immune activation with splenomegaly and lymphadenopathy, activation of T cells with a Th1 profile, and an extreme expansion of TFH cells (Sebastian et al., 2016). KO mice also exhibit a loss of AT, an enlargement of the liver, and hepatic steatosis. To examine the loss of AT more thoroughly in KO mice, a full metabolic study was performed beginning at 4 wk of age. Our previous study showed that at 6 mo of age, KO mice had a complete loss of AT. At 3 mo, AT showed varying degrees of hematopoietic cell infiltrate, ranging from mild to moderate infiltration, to an almost complete destruction of white AT (WAT) (Fig. 1 A). H&E analysis revealed that most male mice had a significant infiltrate and loss of AT, while the onset of adipose loss in female mice was more variable and the ultimate incidence somewhat lower than that of male mice (Fig. 1 A, right panel). Brown AT showed no infiltrate but resembled WAT due to the aberrant storage of lipids (Fig. S1 A). In addition, the pancreas exhibited signs of metabolic dysregulation with islet hyperplasia, a common phenotype in lipoatrophic mice (Fig. S1 B) (Moitra et al., 1998). Although the body weight of wild-type (WT) and KO male mice remained similar with age, and that of female mice was somewhat increased, the fat mass of both male and female KO mice declined over the study period, while the lean mass of both increased (Fig. 1 B). Notably, the liver mass was increased in KO mice and KO mice exhibited elevated liver triglycerides and significant lipid storage that was evident upon H&E staining (Fig. 1 C). The inability of AT to store triglycerides leads to increased lipid accumulations in all insulin-sensitive non-AT, including the liver. This results in insulin resistance, increased circulating insulin, and the stimulation of lipogenesis in hepatocytes, which contributes to hepatic steatosis in lipodystrophic mice (Shimomura et al., 1998; Shimomura et al., 2000). Analysis at the end of the study period showed that at steady state, both female and male KO mice had elevated blood glucose and elevated insulin (Fig. 1 D). Decreased levels of leptin in male KO mice were observed due to the extreme loss of adipocytes (Fig. 1 D). Finally, insulin tolerance tests indicated that KO mice were insulin-resistant (Fig. 1 E). While the KO mice clearly display signs of altered metabolic homeostasis, similar to other models of Treg dysfunction in the AT (Li et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2022), this model appears to be distinct as the metabolic dysfunction is likely secondary to the extensive inflammatory response and profound loss of adipocytes resulting in redistribution of lipid to the liver.Figure 1.

In the absence of Helios, Tregs proceed directly to activated eTregs but with a dysfunctional or exhausted endstage, resulting in a loss of suppressor function.DiscussionT cell activation, effector differentiation, and maintenance of a self-renewing pool are complex processes tightly controlled by transcriptional networks. Tregs can be separated into functionally distinct subsets (resting, activated, effector) based on the differential expression of certain key TFs (Sumida et al., 2024). We demonstrate here that the restriction of Treg effector differentiation, mediated through Id3, Tcf1, Lef1, and Bach2, is globally controlled by the TF Helios. Our findings show that this global regulation of differentiation by Helios is critical in the context of tissue inflammation to maintain a sufficient aTreg pool and to generate a functional eTreg pool to prevent autoimmunity.Treg dysfunction in mice typically results in systemic immune activation and multi-organ leukocyte infiltration and destruction. The organs involved are skin, lungs, liver, colon, salivary glands, and others but, curiously, never AT. It is possible that lipodystrophy is not observed in some models as the mice die at a young age or examination of AT was overlooked. It is unclear why the Helios-deficient mice do not display manifestation of other organ-specific autoimmunity, especially as ST2 and CXCR3 play roles in Treg function in other organs such as skin and lung. We cannot rule out a contribution of mild systemic inflammation in other tissues. In preliminary studies, we have bred the Helios-deficient gene to the BALB/c background. In contrast to C57BL/6 mice, BALB/c mice with Helios-deficient Tregs do show signs of AT loss but die at an early age (10 wk) secondary to autoantibodies to components of the clotting system. Most likely, MHC, background genes, and the TCR repertoire play a role in the development of T effector populations.While Treg function is assumed to be defective in many models of organ-specific autoimmunity, the number of organ-infiltrating Tregs is frequently paradoxically increased. The development of autoimmune lipodystrophy as the only manifestation of organ-specific autoimmunity in mice with Helios-deficient Tregs afforded us the opportunity to examine in depth the cellular and molecular basis of defects in Treg function at a single tissue site. This model resembles patients with autoimmune disease affecting a single organ system. In general, AT Tregs have been shown to be regulators of adipose metabolism (Becker et al., 2017; Panduro et al., 2016). While the autoimmune lipodystrophy shown here exhibits disordered metabolic regulation, our studies demonstrate that the metabolic dysfunction is likely secondary to the destruction of adipocytes by autoreactive T cells, primarily CD8+ T cells. Thus, Tregs not only help to maintain AT metabolic homeostasis but also function in the AT to suppress Th1- and Th2-mediated inflammation and autoimmunity. It should be emphasized that this model of autoimmune lipodystrophy is unusual in that the major pathology is mediated by CD8+ T cells. While CD4+ T cells are present in the AT infiltrates, they do not appear to mediate adipocyte destruction. In preliminary studies, we have observed that the sera from mice with lipodystrophy contain multiple antibodies to adipocytes, including anti-perilipin-1, which has been observed in patients with autoimmune lipodystrophy (Mandel-Brehm et al., 2023). We have not ruled out the possibility that CD4+ T cell helper function is critical for the generation of the CD8+ effector cells in the mouse model.Tcf1, Lef1, Bach2, and Id2/Id3 are the primary core TFs that regulate the development of aTregs while simultaneously restricting eTreg differentiation (Sumida et al., 2024). Mice with deletions of these TFs all exhibit systemic autoimmunity. Miyazaki et al. demonstrated that mice with a Treg-specific deletion of Id2/Id3 develop a fatal Th2-mediated disease with severe inflammation of the skin and lungs (Miyazaki et al., 2014). Splenic Tregs in these dKO mice had increased eTregs characterized by enhanced expression of CXCR5, PD-1, ICOS, CXCR3, CXCR4, and CTLA-4, but significantly reduced Foxp3 expression. Mice with a germline deletion of Bach2 also develop a fatal lung disease, and Tregs in these mice express increased percentages of cells with an eTreg phenotype (Tcf1lo, Lef1lo, with enhanced expression of Klrg1, IRF4, Blimp, T-bet, and Gata3), consistent with a role of Bach2 in the maintenance of T cell quiescence (Kim et al., 2014; Roychoudhuri et al., 2013). Mice with Treg-specific deletion of Tcf7/Lef1 display systemic immune activation and organ-specific autoimmunity (Xing et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2019).In contrast to the core TFs, deletion of TFs primarily involved in eTreg differentiation (Batf, IRF4, Gata3, Blimp, t-bet, PPARγ, and Bcl6) displays variable manifestations of autoimmunity in the steady state. IRF4 and Batf bind promoter regions of ST2 and PPARγ, and mice with a deletion of either IRF4 or Batf lack Tregs in AT and also develop systemic autoimmune disease (Delacher et al., 2020; Vasanthakumar et al., 2015). In contrast, deletions of Gata3 (Wohlfert et al., 2011), Bcl6 (Sawant et al., 2015), t-bet (Levine et al., 2017), and Pparγ (Cipolletta et al., 2012) result in modest or no autoimmunity, while mice with deletions of Stat3 (Chaudhry et al., 2009) or t-bet and Gata-3 combined (Yu et al., 2015) present with systemic activation and organ-specific autoimmunity. Among these TFs, PPARγ is uniquely expressed in AT Tregs and not Tregs from peripheral lymphoid organs (Cipolletta et al., 2012). Strikingly, differences in PPARγ expression were not observed between WT and KO AT Tregs, nor were Helios binding peaks detected in the PPARγ locus, demonstrating that Helios does control PPARγ either directly or indirectly. In contrast to the absence of Helios, the lack of PPARγ in Tregs does not result in autoimmunity, highlighting the myriad of Treg functions and the distinct transcriptional programs that regulate these processes.As Helios globally controls the core TFs that restrain eTreg differentiation, it is quite surprising that Helios KO mice do not exhibit rapid, multi-organ autoimmunity, like that observed in the individual TF-deficient mice. Additional studies are required to determine how Helios fine-tunes the complex expression of these core TFs and the downstream effector TFs and markers.eTregs in the AT also express two unique markers, ST2 and CXCR3 (Torres et al., 2024). The ST2+ and CXCR3+ eTreg populations are postulated to play distinct roles in AT homeostasis. ST2+ eTregs regulate metabolism, and CXCR3+ eTregs regulate inflammation via control of T cell activation. In stark contrast to our studies that demonstrated a marked loss of CXCR3+ eTregs and destruction of adipocytes in Helios KO mice, a reduction in CXCR3+ AT Tregs via the conditional depletion of Tbx21 or Ifng resulted in the loss of CXCR3+ Tregs but did not affect the health or homeostasis of the AT, even though an increase in CD8+ T cells was observed. This result is surprising as we observe an increase in T-bet and IFNγ expression in the KO AT Tregs. This result highlights the complex role of Helios in regulating AT homeostasis and demonstrates that Helios regulates a very specific subset of Treg functions.In contrast to many of the other TFs, Helios is required throughout the entire differentiation process. At the aTreg stage, it directly maintains Id3, Tcf1, Lef1, and Bach2 expression to restrain eTreg differentiation. Notably, in the transcriptome of AT KO Tregs, there is a cluster of genes involved in Wnt signaling that is downregulated (Msx1, FermT2, Ebf2, and Hes1). As Wnt signaling is critical for the generation and maintenance of stem cells with self-renewal properties, as well as effector differentiation (Choi et al., 2015; Gattinoni et al., 2009; Jeannet et al., 2010; Notani et al., 2010), it suggests that Helios may play a role in effector differentiation through this pathway. Helios is also required for the generation of functional eTregs that express CXCR3, ST2, Klrg1, and IRF4. Regulation at this step appears to be indirect, as Helios binding sites were not identified within the chromatin of these genes. Motif enrichment analysis revealed potential regulatory interactions between Helios and members of both the STAT and KLF TF families, as well as Bach2 and BATF. Notably, Klf2, Klf3, and Klf4 were found to be downregulated in KO Tregs, and the absence of Helios may influence the activity of these potential cofactors. However, further studies are required to clarify how Helios controls the terminal differentiation phenotype.It is generally accepted that Ikaros family TFs are transcriptional repressors, but our data demonstrate that in Tregs, Helios acts primarily as a transcriptional activator. Helios has, indeed, been shown to be a repressor in both Tregs (Baine et al., 2013) and hematopoietic stem cells to repress megakaryocytes (Cova et al., 2021). However, in cochlear sensory cells, Helios is a transcriptional activator required for outer hair cell (OHC) maturation (Chessum et al., 2018). In its absence, OHC signature genes are reduced and overexpression of Helios in inner hair cells confers an OHC phenotype. Similarly, Helios acts as a transcriptional activator in leukemic stem cells to promote self-renewal genes and is also required to repress myeloid differentiation (Park et al., 2019). Finally, Aiolos and Ikaros have both been shown to act cooperatively with STAT3 as transcriptional activators of Bcl-6 (Read et al., 2017).Taken together, the role of Helios in Treg differentiation is multifaceted. Upon appropriate signaling in peripheral lymphoid tissues that leads to TFR differentiation, Helios directly maintains Tcf1 and Lef1 expression and indirectly induces TFR-defining genes such as PD-1 and CXCR5. In the absence of Helios, Tcf1 and Lef1 expression is not maintained and TFR are not generated. In the tissue environment, Helios is required to directly maintain Id3, Tcf1, Lef1, and Bach2 expression to inhibit eTreg differentiation. In the absence of Helios, Tregs are not held at the aTreg state, proceed directly to activated eTregs, fail to upregulate Klrg1-, IRF4-, and eTreg-specific markers (ST2/CXCR3), and develop a dysfunctional or exhausted phenotype, with loss of suppressor function.
