TITLE: Oligodendrocyte dysfunction in human age-related cognitive decline

ABSTRACT:
AbstractCognitive decline in aging impacts quality of life, for which therapeutic interventions are lacking. Why some individuals experience more severe cognitive decline with aging is unclear. Here we uncover a role for the brain’s myelin-forming cells—oligodendrocytes—in explaining this variance. By investigating neuropathological and transcriptomic changes in human white matter associated with individual rates of cognitive decline in aging, we find that worse cognitive trajectories unexpectedly associate with smaller myelinated axon size, thicker myelin and more oligodendrocytes with downregulation of NRF2. Aged oligodendrocyte-specific NRF2-knockout mice showed attenuated cognitive improvement over time and mirrored the white matter pathology of human cognitive decline in aging. These findings place the oligodendrocyte as a contributor to cognitive decline and highlight the NRF2 pathway as a therapeutic target to preserve cognition in human aging.

BODY:
MainCognitive decline, defined by impaired memory, reasoning and processing speed over time, is a major socioeconomic burden, rising in prevalence due to the aging population. The underlying mechanisms driving cognitive decline in aging remain unclear. Learning in humans in youth and adulthood is associated with increased central nervous system (CNS) myelin1, the insulating membrane produced by oligodendrocytes that surrounds neuronal axons to facilitate rapid electrical impulse conduction and provide metabolic support. Furthermore, it is now well established that, in mice, learning requires an increase in oligodendrocytes through de novo generation2, 3, 4, 5–6. Cognitive impairment in aged mice and non-human primates is associated with reduced oligodendrocytes and myelin6,7; however, the white matter pathologies driving human cognitive decline remain unclear. Indeed, although magnetic resonance imaging studies associate white matter abnormalities with cognitive decline in aged humans8, 9, 10, 11–12, the underpinning oligodendrocyte and myelin changes are unknown. To assess gross changes in myelin, we used spectral confocal reflectance (SCoRe) microscopy to specifically detect compacted myelin22, which showed a significant decrease in individuals with severe cognitive decline (Fig. 1e–g). To assess myelin integrity and myelinated axonal health on an ultra-structural level, corpus callosum samples from a subset of LBC1936 individuals were processed for transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 1h). No differences were observed in the density or percentage of myelinated axons (Fig. 1i,j). However, individuals with more severe cognitive decline had a shift in myelinated axon profiles toward smaller axon diameters, as indicated by probability plots of the frequency distribution and average myelinated axon size (Fig. 1k−m). Myelin thickness was surprisingly increased with worse cognitive decline, measured by a reduced g ratio (ratio of axon diameter: axon + myelin diameter) but only for large-diameter axons (>1 µm) (Fig. 1n,o). Consistent with decreased SCoRe signal, cases with severe cognitive decline had increased myelin decompactions in large-diameter axons (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d); however, these did not artificially impact myelin thickness measurements as there was no significant relationship to g ratio (R = −0.36, P = 0.22).We also noted several instances where myelinated profiles were present but had no apparent axon (labeled ‘empty’; Fig. 1p), suggestive of the early stages of Wallerian degeneration23, which increased in frequency with worse cognitive decline (Fig. 1q,r). The degenerating and smaller axon profiles alongside thicker myelin could be indicative of a primary axonopathy, such as axon shrinkage. However, we did not observe any myelinated axons with established features known to precede axonopathy, such as axonal spheroids or condensed (dark) axoplasm24,25. In addition, average mitochondrial size, known to increase with axonal stress especially in large-diameter myelinated axons26,27, was not correlated with severity of cognitive decline yet was increased generally with aging, as expected28 (Fig. 1s,t). Furthermore, no difference was observed in the percentage of area positive for the axon pathology marker SMI-32 (ref. 29) between cases with mild and severe cognitive decline (Fig. 1u,v).We acknowledge that many clinical parameters and technical artifacts from tissue processing are often confounding factors in human tissue analyses. Nonetheless, neither axon diameter, myelin thickness nor the density of degenerating myelinated axon profiles was associated with age at death, sex, age 11 IQ, technical variables (such as postmortem interval, brain weight, brain pH) or neuropathological indices of small vessel disease (Extended Data Fig. 1b). Moreover, we did not see a significant relationship between myelinated axon changes and absolute cognition (either at age 70 or extrapolated at death; Extended Data Fig. 1b). Therefore, it is worsened cognitive decline over time, rather than absolute cognition, that is associated with myelinated axon pathology. Interestingly, there was a significant negative association between Braak stage and axon diameter (Extended Data Fig. 1b), suggesting that smaller axons may be a shared pathological feature between age-associated cognitive decline and Alzheimer’s disease pathology. To understand how oligodendrocyte subclusters were altered with cognitive decline, pseudobulking and differential gene expression analyses were performed using edgeR and limma packages; genetic variance associated with technical factors (library size) and/or biological factors already known to influence cognition (age, sex, IQ at age 70 and histopathological evidence of Alzheimer’s disease or small vessel disease) were regressed out as covariates. Of all the upstream pathway regulators identified as significant by IPA (|Z| > 2), the only one shared across both Oligo4 and Oligo5 was NRF2 (Z = 2.217 and Z = 2.183 for Oligo4 and Oligo5, respectively), which was predicted to be downregulated with more severe cognitive decline in both subpopulations (Supplementary Data 1). To assess whether these responses are functionally linked, we asked whether oligodendrocyte accumulation alone would be sufficient to induce downregulation of NRF2. TAM, tamoxifen.First, we performed the open field test to screen for any major motor or anxiety effects that may influence cognitive test readouts. Female conditional knockout mice showed confounding altered anxiety levels (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b) and were, thus, excluded from cognitive assay analysis. Next, the Morris water maze (MWM) was used to assess cognitive performance and learning over time, facilitating comparison to the longitudinal cognitive testing in the human cohort. Notably, the MWM has previously been shown to be sensitive to age-related cognitive impairment concomitant with alterations in corpus callosum myelin5,6. Mice underwent an acclimatization period of eight training trials per day for 3 days (four trial patterns, repeated twice), where they swam to a visible platform that changed quadrants between trials (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 4c,d). To then test development of spatial learning and memory, the mice underwent a testing phase of six trials per day for 4 days (three trial patterns, repeated twice), where visual cues enabled localization of the hidden platform in a fixed location (Fig. 3d). Over the course of the testing phase, all mice showed an increase in the percentage of time spent in the target quadrant containing the platform, indicating spatial learning and memory encoding (Fig. 3e). Although conditional knockout animals initially spent more time in the target quadrant in the first 2 days of the testing phase relative to controls, they spent relatively less time in the target quadrant by days 3 and 4 (Fig. 3e). Accordingly, when percentage time spent in the target quadrant was plotted as a fold change relative to testing day 1, controls showed an average improvement of 57.75%, whereas conditional knockouts improved by an average of only 27.82% (Fig. 3f). Conditional knockouts surprisingly had a shorter latency to reach the target quadrant versus controls over all test days (Extended Data Fig. 4e). As this did not reflect faster swim speed in the testing phase (Extended Data Fig. 4f), we next scored search strategy using an established rubric53, where more direct pathways to the platform are associated with higher scores (Extended Data Fig. 4g). Although conditional knockouts showed a more direct spatial strategy from day 1 (Extended Data Fig. 4h), they did not improve their spatial strategy over time as much as the controls (Extended Data Fig. 4i); notably, this did not represent reaching a ceiling level of improvement, as both conditional knockout and control animals only ever reached the maximum score of 6 in approximsately 10% of test trials (Extended Data Fig. 4g).Although conditional knockouts showed attenuated cognitive improvement over time, we noted a large degree of individual variation in performance across both genotypes. To correlate individual cognitive performance with white matter histology as was done in humans, we binned animals based on whether performance over time was better or poorer than average (Fig. 3g). No significant difference was observed in oligodendrocyte densities between better versus poorer performers at 12 months (Fig. 3h). However, when tissue was assessed by electron microscopy, there were clear differences in ultra-structural features of myelinated axons between better and poorer performers (Fig. 3i). Both axon diameter and g ratio were significantly reduced in poorer performers (Fig. 3j,k), indicating that smaller axons and thicker myelin are correlates of worse cognitive performance in aging mice. In late aging (15−18 months), oligodendrocyte density and myelin compaction were unchanged (Extended Data Fig. 4j−n); however, myelinated axon diameter and g ratio were significantly reduced in conditional knockouts versus controls (Fig. 3l−o). This suggests that loss of NRF2 in oligodendrocytes exacerbates the neuropathological features associated with age-related cognitive decline. These findings demonstrate that NRF2 downregulation in oligodendrocytes is sufficient to blunt cognitive performance over time in aging and induce thicker myelin and a shift to smaller myelinated axon diameters, mirroring observations in severe cognitive decline in human aging. Altogether, these results indicate a novel role for oligodendroglial NRF2 in contributing to cognitive impairment in aging.DiscussionHere we identify the oligodendrocyte as a contributor to the neuropathology of cognitive decline with aging. Using a unique human brain bank from a cohort with lifelong individual trajectories of cognitive status complemented by functional modeling in transgenic mice, we reveal that oligodendrocyte dysfunction contributes to myelinated axon pathology and cognitive impairment over time in aging. This study suggests that oligodendrocytes are not always supportive of CNS function and cognition, revealing that they can become actively detrimental in aging.We found that individuals with severe cognitive decline have a loss of myelin integrity, including abnormally thicker myelin on large-diameter axons and a shift toward smaller myelinated axon profiles. These may directly influence cognitive ability, as 85% of variance in human axon conduction velocity is predicted to be controlled by myelin thickness and axon size54. Nonetheless, our association of more myelin with worse cognitive decline is surprising, given that, in youth/adulthood, learning involves myelin production1,55, and cognitive deficits are typically associated with myelin loss56,57. This could reflect that aging-associated cognitive decline involves surpassing the amount of additional myelin which is beneficial. Indeed, excess myelin thickening is known to precede CNS neurodegeneration in young adults with multiple sclerosis58, in aged non-human primates59 and in mouse models of amyloidopathy37,60, 61–62. Consistent with these findings, myelinated axons are recently recognized to be more vulnerable to degeneration than non-myelinated axons in human24 and mouse white matter63, indicating that pathological myelin is more deleterious for axons than no myelin at all. We build upon this by now identifying a particular vulnerability of myelinated axons of large diameter (>1 µm) in human aging, which preferentially show thicker myelin and decreased frequencies in more severe cognitive decline. This contrasts with previous work documenting decreased small-diameter myelinated axons in human white matter in aging versus youth64, 65–66; we did not detect this phenomenon in association with cognitive decline, suggesting that small axon pathology may occur at an equal rate across aged individuals yet could have less impact on cognitive decline than large axon pathology.We uncovered that oligodendrocyte dysfunction can be driven by NRF2 deficiency. Decreased expression of NRF2 in oligodendrocytes was observed in individuals with severe cognitive decline; modeling this in mice was functionally sufficient to cause thicker myelin and a shift toward myelination of smaller-diameter axons, and to blunt cognitive improvement over time in aging. NRF2 regulates the activation of hundreds of genes in a cell-type-specific manner, in turn influencing a wide range of cellular functions, including autophagy, antioxidant responses, proliferation and differentiation; here we identify a novel role for NRF2 in oligodendrocytes in regulating myelin thickness and myelinated axon health. Although the exact mechanism by which NRF2 regulates myelin thickness is unknown, the established interplay between NRF2 and autophagy in other cell and tissue systems suggests that this could contribute and would be consistent with autophagy disruption in oligodendrocytes leading to hypermyelination and subsequent neurodegeneration67, 68–69. This complements the well-established role of NRF2 in cognition, where global NRF2 knockout exacerbates age-related cognitive deficits in several mouse models41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48–49; drug-induced NRF2 activation (using dimethyl fumarate) improves cognitive function in rodents and in multiple sclerosis70, 71, 72, 73–74, yet the contributing cell type was undefined. Together with our findings, this highlights the possibility of repurposing NRF2 activators to mitigate oligodendrocyte dysfunction associated with cognitive decline in human aging.There are likely several triggers of oligodendrocyte dysfunction and consequent myelin pathology in aging. Our data suggest that an increase in oligodendrocytes may occur upstream of NRF2 downregulation. Whether the increased oligodendrocytes in more severe cognitive decline reflects new generation in aging or enhanced generation earlier in life is unknown, although limited oligodendrogenesis in human aging75 may support the latter possibility. Our finding of a deleterious association of oligodendrocyte densities with cognitive status is unexpected, as new oligodendrocyte generation is required for learning during development and adulthood in mice2, 3, 4–5, and supraphysiological boosting of new oligodendrocytes in aged mice can attenuate age-related memory deficits4,6,76. Nevertheless, increased oligodendrocyte densities have been previously documented in human pathology—in the gray matter in Alzheimer’s disease77 and in the white matter after developmental insults78— although the functional status of these oligodendrocytes and the consequences of this increase remain unexplored in these contexts. Our findings suggest that, at least in the context of human aging, an increase in oligodendrocytes is associated with adoption of a state that is dysfunctional. There are likely also other upstream drivers of oligodendrocyte dysfunction and myelin pathology in aging; changes in the aging brain, such as inflammation and vascular pathology, are known modulators of NRF2 (refs. 79,80) and oligodendrocyte/myelin biology81,82 and may, therefore, contribute to cognitive decline. In addition, an established driver of increased myelin thickness is neuronal activity, which drives experience-dependent adaptive myelin remodeling to support learning83,84. Neuronal activity is suggested to increase in aging in some individuals with detrimental outcomes for lifespan85, and we speculate that this may also support maladaptive (excessive) myelination86 to contribute to cognitive decline. Aging humans often show greater activity in certain brain regions during cognitive tasks compared to younger individuals87, 88–89, which may reflect an increase in neuronal activity in an attempt to compensate for cognitive decline. Investigation of other brain regions beyond the corpus callosum could, therefore, reveal additional cellular and molecular contributors to cognitive decline in aging.Altogether, our study reveals the oligodendrocyte to be a contributor to the white matter neuropathology associated with cognitive decline in aging. The MWM consisted of a circular water bath (diameter 1.5 m) filled with water and a non-toxic white dye that discolored the water, and was divided into four quadrants corresponding to ordinal directions (NE/SE/SW/NW) with visible cues (91.4 cm wide) on the wall at each direction of the maze (N/E/S/W). MWM testing took place over 9 days, consisting of a 3-day acclimatization phase, a 2-day rest period and then a 4-day testing phase. During acclimatization, a visible platform (20 × 3 × 25.4 cm) was submerged but identifiable via projecting pillars. On each day, animals were tested in two sessions, with four trials per session (total of eight trials per day per animal). In each trial, mice were allowed to navigate to the platform for up to 1 minute and removed upon successful navigation to the platform. If a mouse ceased swimming and floated on its back, the mouse was removed and the trial was ended. At the end of the trial, the platform changed location; the order of which quadrant the mouse was placed in, to where the visible platform was located, was NE to NW; SE to SW; SW to NE; and NE to SE. Next, during testing, a hidden platform (19.05 cm in diameter) was submerged without visible pillars and remained in the same location in the NE quadrant. On each day, animals were tested in two sessions, with three trials per session (total of six trials per day per animal), and were placed in different starting quadrants corresponding to the following order: SE, NW, SW. As above, in each trial, animals were allowed to navigate to the platform for up to 1 minute, removed upon successful navigation to the platform and removed/trial ended if an animal ceased swimming and floated on its back. If an animal failed to reach the platform after 1 minute (in either phase), they were placed on the platform for another minute. During all trials, investigators remained visibly hidden to the animals by stepping behind a curtain. WaterMaze6 software (Actimetrics) recorded mouse location, and experimenters measured time to reach the platform with a manual timer.
