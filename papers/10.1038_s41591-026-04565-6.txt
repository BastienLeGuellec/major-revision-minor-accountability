TITLE: Atezolizumab in early triple-negative breast cancer: the randomized phase 3 NSABP B-59/GeparDouze trial

ABSTRACT:
AbstractTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype with an activated tumor immune microenvironment. The multicenter, multinational, double-blinded NSABP B-59/GeparDouze trial evaluated the addition of atezolizumab (atezo) (773 patients randomized) or placebo (777 patients) to sequential taxane–carboplatin–anthracycline-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy in stage II–III TNBC. The addition of atezo did not significantly improve the primary endpoint of event-free survival (EFS) (HR, 0.80 (95% CI, 0.062–1.03); stratified log-rank P = 0.083, 4-year EFS rates difference 3.3%). The HR for overall survival was 0.86 (95% CI, 0.62–1.19), with a 4-year benefit of 0.7%. Prespecified subgroup analyses suggested heterogeneity in EFS, with benefit of atezo in patients presenting with clinical lymph node involvement (Pinteraction = 0.039). Treatment-emergent adverse events with grades ≥3 were reported in 75.3% (atezo) versus 73.4% (placebo), and immune-related adverse events were reported in 27.6% (atezo) versus 11.4% (placebo). A total of 196 (25.5%) patients discontinued atezo and 143 (18.8%) patients discontinued placebo in the neoadjuvant phase. In an exploratory mRNA-based subset analysis, patients with basal-like immune-activated tumors may have benefited from atezo. TNBC subtyping to identify basal-like immune-activated tumors and quantification of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes to identify tumors with high tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte counts could be a promising strategy to identify patients who benefit from the addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. ClinicalTrials.gov registration: NCT03281954.

BODY:
MainTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive and relatively immunogenic subtype with potential susceptibility to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)1,2. Several neoadjuvant studies in early TNBC have shown a moderately increased pathological complete response (pCR) rate of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) with ICIs3,4. In addition to pCR improvement, KEYNOTE-522 showed a statistically significant improvement in event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS), irrespective of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) status, with the addition of anti-PD-1 antibody pembrolizumab to chemotherapy5,6.Atezolizumab, an anti-PD-L1 antibody, significantly improved progression-free survival when added to nab-paclitaxel for the first-line treatment of metastatic, PD-L1-positive TNBC7. It also improved the pCR rate of early-stage TNBC with a trend for improved long-term outcomes when added to taxane–anthracycline-based NACT8.NSABP B-59/GeparDouze (NCT03281954) was an international, double-blind, placebo-controlled, academic phase 3 clinical trial which investigated whether the addition of atezolizumab to neoadjuvant taxane–carboplatin–anthracycline-based chemotherapy would improve outcomes for patients with stages II–III centrally confirmed TNBC. The study was developed as a contemporary to the global KEYNOTE-522 trial. The study design is shown in Extended Data Fig. 1.In this Article, we present the primary analysis (4-year EFS results) and secondary analyses (pCR, distant disease-free survival (DDFS), OS, safety) of the NSABP B-59/GeparDouze trial along with exploratory translational investigations focusing on the prognostic and predictive potential of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). In a prospectively planned sub-study, mRNA analysis and TNBC molecular subtyping of primary tumors were integrated. An exploratory analysis of the outcomes by mRNA-based TNBC subtypes was implemented and compared with the TIL results.ResultsPatients and therapyBetween 29 December 2017 and 28 May 2021, 2,188 patients were screened and 1,550 patients with centrally confirmed TNBC were enrolled across 353 sites in 4 countries (USA, Germany, Canada and Spain), with 773 patients randomly assigned to receive neoadjuvant atezolizumab 1,200 mg IV and 777 patients to receive placebo IV every 3 weeks concurrently with NACT. Chemotherapy was composed of paclitaxel 80 mg m−2 IV weekly × 12 doses with carboplatin area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) of 5 IV on day 1 and every 3 weeks for 4 cycles, followed by doxorubicin (anthracyclin, A) 60 mg m−2 IV or epirubicin (E) 90 mg m−2 IV with cyclophosphamide (C) 600 mg m−2 IV on day 1 and every 2 weeks or 3 weeks per investigator discretion for 4 cycles (AC and EC, respectively). This gene signature has been subsequently extended to a larger set of differentially expressed genes11. This analysis also showed differences of TNBC subtypes in the overall pCR rate and EFS as well as a heterogenous distribution of TNBC6 subtypes in tumors with intermediate TILs (Extended Data Figs. 5 and 6).Heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment on the gene expression levelIn the previous translational program of the neoadjuvant GeparNuevo (G9) trial, we had identified three G9 signatures relevant for response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy9. As shown in Extended Data Fig. 7a,b, in the NSABP B-59/GeparDouze biomarker cohort, these gene signatures were differentially expressed across the three TIL subgroups as well as the TNBC4-IR subtypes. BLIA tumors as well as tumors with high TILs had increased levels of the G9 immune signature and reduced levels of the G9-stromal signature. For the G9 proliferation signature, only minor differences were observed between the different TNBC subtypes and TIL-related subgroups.We performed an analysis of differential gene expression and a gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) in different TIL subgroups as well as the BLIA and BLIS subgroups. This analysis showed a significant enrichment of immune-related pathways and genes in tumors with high TILs (Extended Data Fig. 7c–h). By contrast, stromal gene expression, including the transforming growth factor (TGF)-beta pathway, was significantly higher in tumors with low TILs. There were also considerable differences in gene expression in the different TNBC subtypes, suggesting relevant differences in cellular interactions in the tumor microenvironment. The BLIA and BLIS subtypes showed inverse expression patterns of immune genes, which are overexpressed in the BLIA subtype and reduced in the BLIS subtype (Extended Data Fig. 7i). In parallel, stromal biomarkers including the TGF-beta pathway were increased in the BLIS subtype and decreased in the BLIA subtype (Extended Data Fig. 7j). This suggests that the balance between immune activation and stromal organization in the tumor microenvironment is relevant for the response to immunotherapy.In an additional exploratory subgroup analysis, we evaluated TILs as well as TNBC4-IR subtypes in patient subgroups stratified by nodal status, based on the clinical observation that high TILs and positive nodal status determine subgroups with a significant atezolizumab effect. The effect of TILs on EFS and pCR was comparable among nodal-positive and nodal-negative tumors (Extended Data Fig. 8).Interestingly, the distribution of TILs as well as TNBC4-IR subtypes was different in the nodal-positive and the nodal-negative subsets of patients in the biomarker cohort. As shown in Extended Data Fig. 9, in the nodal-positive subgroup, there were more tumors with high TILs (35.3% versus 28.8%) and more tumors with intermediate TILs (48.8% versus 40.2%) than in the node-negative group. By contrast, in the node-positive subgroup, only 15.9% of patients had low TILs, compared with 31% in the nodal-negative subset. In the nodal-positive subgroup, 59.2% of patients had a BLIA subtype, compared with only 46.6% in the nodal-negative subgroup. Therefore, the higher rate of immune-activated tumors in the nodal-positive subgroup could explain the increased effect of atezolizumab in this subgroup. The KM analysis of different subtypes in nodal-positive and negative tumors is shown in Extended Data Fig. 10.DiscussionThe NSABP B-59/GeparDouze study investigated the addition of atezolizumab to a standard taxane–carboplatin–anthracycline-based NACT regimen. At the definitive analysis of the primary endpoint, the 4-year EFS was 85.2% with atezolizumab and 81.9% with placebo (stratified HR, 0.800 (95% CI, 0.621–1.030); stratified log-rank test P = 0.083), which did not meet the prespecified boundary for statistical significance and claiming superiority. Exploratory prespecified subgroup analyses of EFS showed heterogeneity across subgroups, suggesting potential benefit of atezolizumab in subgroups such as those with nodal involvement at presentation, those with primary tumors >3 cm and those with tumor TILs ≥30%. Exploratory mRNA-based subtyping also suggested an atezolizumab effect in patients whose tumors were TIL enriched and of the BLIA subtype, while patients whose tumors had intermediate TIL levels showed heterogeneity of the tumor immune microenvironment with no suggestion of atezolizumab benefit.The result of the secondary endpoint of DDFS was similar, with a 4-year DDFS rate of 87.2% with atezolizumab and 85.2% with placebo (HR, 0.834 (95% CI, 0.633–1.098)). OS analysis was immature, but the 4-year OS was favorable in both arms, 90.2% versus 89.5%, respectively (HR, 0.86 (95% CI, 0.62–1.19)).The absolute increase in the secondary endpoint of the pCR rate of 6.3% (57% with placebo to 63.3% with atezolizumab) is in line with previously reported data3, 4–5,8,13. Notably, the pCR rate in the NACT-placebo group is one of the highest reported without ICI therapy and patients who experience pCR have a favorable outcome regardless of ICI therapy.In contrast to KEYNOTE-522, our study did not show a significant improvement in EFS and OS by adding a PD-L1 inhibitor to an optimal NACT regimen. There are important differences between the two trials. Firstly, the ICI investigated in both trials is different—the PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab was used in KEYNOTE-522 while the PD-L1 inhibitor atezolizumab was used in this trial. Secondly, two-thirds of the patients in this trial received dose-dense EC or AC, which was not allowed in KEYNOTE-522, and 50% of the patients with non-pCR in the NSABP B-59/GeparDouze trial received capecitabine as adjuvant therapy. Finally, the overall risk profile accrued to the respective trials appeared to be lower in the NSABP B-59/GeparDouze study. For instance, clinically node-positive patients comprised 51% of the KEYNOTE-522 population compared with 42% in this study. Non-pCR patients receiving placebo and no capecitabine in NSABP B-59/GeparDouze had a 3-year EFS rate of 70.2% compared with 56.8% in KEYNOTE-522. In general, a lower-risk population has a lower event rate, and therefore, it is difficult to detect the same benefit on a statistically significant level.The description of TILs as a marker for increased pCR rates after neoadjuvant therapy and as a prognostic marker for survival outcome has been validated in many previous studies14, 15, 16, 17, 18–19. The NSABP B-59/GeparDouze trial incorporated prospective central evaluation of TILs in randomized patients by trained pathologists at the central pathology sites for the study groups NSABP and GBG following the standardized recommendations of the International TILs Working Group18, to evaluate the specific role of TILs as a potential marker of immunotherapy benefit20. Our study confirms the prognostic role of TILs as well as a marker for an increased pCR rate in tumors with higher TILs. Exploratory analysis also suggested that patients’ high-TIL tumors (defined as ≥30%) may have had a favorable effect on EFS with atezolizumab compared with placebo. This suggests that high levels of TILs might be able to identify a subgroup of patients with increased benefit from ICI in early TNBC. Our parallel gene expression analysis shows that tumors with high TIL levels consist predominantly of the BLIA subtype, with an increased expression of many immune-related genes.In contrast to this high-TIL group, the potential clinical utility of TIL assessment is currently limited in tumors with intermediate TILs. Our gene expression analysis shows considerable heterogeneity in this subgroup, with a mixture of BLIS and BLIA subtypes. Consequently, no difference between the atezolizumab arm and the placebo arm was observed in this subgroup with intermediate TILs. Therefore, it might be useful to use other approaches, in particular gene expression-based subtyping, for patients with tumors with intermediate TILs. Interestingly, this biomarker approach would be very similar to the situation for proliferation markers and gene expression testing in luminal breast cancer: on the basis of the recommendation of the International Ki67 in Breast Cancer Working Group, Ki-67 values of 30% or more or of 5% or less could be used for chemotherapy-related therapy decisions, while the intermediate range of Ki-67 between 5% and 30% would require additional gene expression analysis21.In summary, integration of quantifying TILs with RNA-based subtyping to identify the immune-activated BLIA subtype and the high-TIL group identified cohorts with apparent improved outcomes with atezolizumab, suggesting that a pretherapeutic immunologically ‘hot’ microenvironment might be an important predictor of benefit.It should also be emphasized that, in addition to the observed differences in benefit of atezolizumab, there were large differences in neoadjuvant response and survival in the different molecular subtypes of TNBC, observed with both the TNBC4-IR and the TNBC6 approaches in a remarkably similar pattern. This suggests that it might be time to remove TNBC as a ‘title of convenience’22 from breast cancer classification systems and to replace it with a standardized biological classification based on gene expression profiling. This might be essential for the next generation of clinical trials addressing the molecular diversity of the tumor microenvironment in the former TNBC subtype. Our gene expression analysis shows a consistent activation of pathways related to stromal reorganization such as the TGF-beta pathway in tumors with low TILs as well as in the BLIS subtype. The therapeutic interaction with this pathway might provide options to improve response to immunotherapy in this critical subtype.In our exploratory analysis of node-positive and node-negative tumors, we observed higher TIL levels and more immune-activated tumors (BLIA subtype) in the node-positive subgroup. This observation could explain the apparent differential effect of atezolizumab by nodal status in NSABP B-59/GeparDouze. While there are relevant statistical limitations for these exploratory subgroup comparisons, our observations raise the interesting immunological hypothesis whether the interaction between tumor cells and immune cells in the lymph node tissue might be relevant for immunotherapy response as an additional systemic activation of the immune system.Nodal status could reflect differences in antigen exposure or priming in the tumor-draining lymph nodes. In a node-positive tumor, the tumor cells have direct contact with the systemic immune system, and there might be additional immune cells entering the tumor microenvironment that have been stimulated against the tumor cells in the lymph node. Therefore, in the node-positive situation, the immune system has more options to replenish exhausted immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, resulting in a more activated immune system and a higher response rate to atezolizumab. This could be particularly relevant in subgroups with heterogenous immune status, such as the intermediate-TIL group, which might include tumors with an exhausted immune system. In this exhausted milieu, TILs may be present, but are not functional against the tumor cells, and might not be activated by ICI treatment. It would be of interest to investigate this hypothesis in additional cohorts and prospective translational and preclinical investigations.There are limitations relevant to this translational analysis: we have only been able to analyze the gene expression in a subcohort of 482 patients, and even in this comparably large subset, there are smaller subgroups that limit the statistical analyses and the interpretation. We therefore focused our analyses on the most predominant subtypes. Due to the use of the HTG platform for mRNA analysis, we also had to adjust the approaches for determination of the subtypes to the 2,549 genes that are included in the HTG OBP panel. It would be very interesting to evaluate the TNBC4-IR subtypes in additional clinical trial cohorts, including trials with different types of immune-checkpoint inhibitors.In conclusion, the NSABP B-59/GeparDouze study did not show a statistically significant improvement in EFS with atezolizumab, although a significant but small increase in pCR was shown. Exploratory studies suggested potential benefit from atezolizumab in patients with the immune-activated BLIA subtype and in those with high TILs and nodal-positive disease. These findings highlight the potential role of an activated tumor immune microenvironment in predicting a therapeutic benefit with atezolizumab and potentially other ICIs in TNBC.MethodsEthical approval and consentThe trial complied with good clinical practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol and amendments were approved by the competent authorities and independent ethics committees, which are listed in Supplementary Information 1. An independent data monitoring committee oversaw the study. The patients provided written informed consent. There was no compensation for participants.Trial designNSABP B-59/GeparDouze was a multicenter, multinational, prospectively randomized, double-blind placebo-controlled phase 3 trial (NCT03281954) which investigated whether the addition of atezolizumab to NACT followed by adjuvant atezolizumab to complete 1 year of therapy improved EFS in patients with early centrally confirmed TNBC designed by the NSABP, GBG and Investigational Drug Development Institute (IDDI) and funded by grants from Genentech/Roche to the NSABP Foundation. The academic study was led by NSABP and GBG in collaboration with the Spanish Breast Cancer Group (Grupo Español de Investigación es Cáncer de Mama (GEICAM)), US Oncology Research (USOR) and Translational Research in Oncology (TRIO). The study design is shown in Extended Data Fig. 1. The study protocol, a summary of clinically important protocol amendments, and the statistical plan are provided as Supplementary Information 2–4. No further follow-up phase is planned for the study. This academic-led trial was designed, conducted and analyzed by NSABP and GBG in collaboration with IDDI and Roche/Genentech. GEICAM, TRIO and USOR supported recruitment.PatientsEligible patients had clinical stage II–III early TNBC, defined as estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PgR) status below 1% and negative HER2 status. Central testing for HER2, ER and PgR was required to confirm TNBC. For patients with clinical regional lymph node involvement—defined as cN1 with cytological or histological confirmation, or cN2–N3 with or without a biopsy—the primary breast tumor could be clinically staged as T1c, T2 or T3. For patients with clinically negative nodes, the primary tumor had to be T2 or T3.Bilateral or multicentric HER2-negative breast cancers were permitted if the highest-risk tumor was ER negative and PgR negative and met stage eligibility criteria. Patients were also required to have a left ventricular ejection fraction >55% and no important cardiac history. Patients were excluded if they had a history of ipsilateral invasive BC or ipsilateral ductal carcinoma in situ, evidence of metastatic disease, prior treatment for the currently diagnosed BC before randomization, or if they had received immunotherapy previously.Patients were eligible for the study regardless of sex or gender, and sex was determined based on self-report at the time of enrollment. As only one male patient was included in the study, no sex- or gender-based analyses were performed, and study results are reported for the overall cohort. Data were not disaggregated for privacy reasons.Randomization and treatmentThe study was designed as a double-blind trial; neither the patient nor the investigator knew which treatment had been assigned. At the time of protocol activation, patients were stratified by region (North America versus Europe), tumor size (1.1–3.0 cm versus >3.0 cm), AC or EC schedule (q2w versus q3w) and clinical nodal status (positive versus negative) and then centrally randomized 1:1 using permuted block randomization to receive atezolizumab or placebo (1,200 mg IV every 3 weeks).PD-L1 status (positive, negative, indeterminate or not available) was added as a 5th stratification factor by amendment number 1 (16 July 2019) after 374 patients had been randomized. PD-L1 status assessment was done centrally using the antibody Ventana PD-L1 (SP142); the immune cell (IC) score was reported as a percentage according to the Ventana evaluation protocol. A PD-L1 positive status was defined as ≥1% staining of tumor-infiltrating immune cells as a percentage of the tumor area using the SP142 Ventana assay (Ventana Medical Systems).The TIL analysis was performed based on the standardized recommendations of the international TIL working group18. The analysis was performed by trained pathologists at the two central pathology sites for NSABP and GBG. The predefined statistical analysis plan included the TIL cutoff of 10% as well as the cutoff of 30%, based on a large analysis of TILs in TNBC23.All patients were to receive weekly paclitaxel 80 mg m−2 for 12 doses plus carboplatin AUC of 5 every 3 weeks for 4 doses followed by either doxorubicin 60 mg m−2 or epirubicin 90 mg m−2 with cyclophosphamide 600 mg m−2 (AC or EC) every 2–3 weeks for 4 doses (per investigator’s discretion). After surgery (performed within 6 weeks of the last CT dose), the pathologic response status was assessed by the site pathologists, with an additional centrally blinded evaluation of histopathology reports. Patients who did not discontinue atezolizumab or placebo owing to toxicity during neoadjuvant therapy resumed the original randomized investigational therapy assigned until 1 year after the initial dose of atezolizumab or placebo. Radiotherapy, if indicated, was co-administered with atezolizumab or placebo.Post-neoadjuvant systemic therapy other than atezolizumab or placebo was not allowed at the initiation of the trial, but results of the CREATE-X24 and OlympiA25 trials, subsequently changing the standard treatment landscape, required amendments during the accrual phase of the trial to allow administration of adjuvant capecitabine with atezolizumab or placebo for patients with non-pCR status at surgery. Olaparib was allowed for patients with non-pCR status and germline pathogenic variants in BRCA1/2 but required discontinuation of atezolizumab or placebo.EndpointsThe primary endpoint was EFS, defined in accordance with NeoSTEEP26 as time from randomization to the occurrence of any of the following events: disease progression on protocol therapy resulting in the initiation of non-protocol cancer therapy or inoperability, local invasive recurrence following mastectomy, local invasive recurrence in the ipsilateral breast after lumpectomy, regional recurrence, distant recurrence, contralateral invasive breast cancer, a second non-breast primary malignancy or death from any cause. EFS provides earlier and clinically relevant assessment of treatment efficacy and is an established endpoint that has supported regulatory decision-making in the neoadjuvant setting.Secondary endpoints included pCR in the breast and lymph nodes evaluated after surgery (ypT0/Tis ypN0) defined as the absence of any invasive residual tumor in the resected breast specimen and lymph nodes following completion of neoadjuvant therapy; OS, defined as the time from randomization to death due to any reason; DDFS, defined as time from randomization until distant recurrence, death from breast cancer, death from other causes or occurrence of a second primary invasive non-breast cancer; and disease-free survival, defined as the time from first breast surgery to disease recurrence or death from any cause.Safety by toxicity grades was defined by the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.0. Cardiac safety was evaluated in the first 60 patients randomized and who initiated AC or EC in the cardiac safety lead-in cohort. The evaluation was based on the frequency of elevated troponin-T levels and changes in left ventricular ejection fraction, to identify and further assess potential cardiac toxicity associated with the combination of anthracycline and atezolizumab. Brain metastases-free survival was an exploratory endpoint.Study assessmentsEFS was evaluated by the investigator in accordance with national guidelines. pCR was assessed by a local pathologist following completion of NACT and confirmed by central assessment of histopathology reports. All adverse events, including SAEs, were monitored throughout the trial and for 30 days after the discontinuation of study treatment. Adverse events of special interest were defined by a protocol-specified list of terms and were classified as adverse events of special interest only if they met the corresponding predefined severity grading criteria. These events were monitored—regardless of their relationship to the study drug—from the first dose through follow-up, until study closure.Translational biomarker programGene expression analysis using the HTG OBP panelGene expression was profiled in 494 FFPE tumor tissues using the HTG OBP (HTG Molecular Diagnostics) that measures the expression of 2,549 genes associated with tumor biology. For quality control of HTG measurements, the mean of four negative and four positive internal controls was calculated for each sample. The measurement was repeated for a sample if the mean of its positive controls was below two standard deviations (s.d.s) of the grand mean across all samples or if the mean of its negative controls was above two s.d.s from the grand mean. As additional quality control, samples with less than 1.5 million total counts were excluded from further analysis. The quality-controlled data were normalized using the counts per million (CPM) method and log2 transformation. Finally, to improve reproducibility, a modification of the CPM method was applied by introducing a lower bound of 3 on CPM values as reported previously9. All subsequent translational analyses involving gene expression and TNBC subtypes were conducted post hoc and are therefore considered exploratory.Molecular subtyping of TNBCTNBC6 subtypingWe developed a TNBC6 subtype classification algorithm using the gene signatures previously defined12,27. The algorithm was adapted to the HTG OBP, which provided coverage for 1,070 genes overlapping with the published TNBC6 signatures. Our algorithm classified all samples into one of the six TNBC subtypes (BL1, BL2, IM, M, MSL and LAR), and care was taken to avoid assigning samples to an ‘unstable’ category.Transcriptomic data of these 1,070 overlapping genes from 180 TNBC samples in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), each with previously reported TNBC6 subtype assignments27, were used as the reference set. Gene expression values were log2 transformed and standardized using z-transformation (mean = 0, s.d. = 1) to ensure comparability across samples. The resulting z-scored matrix was used to derive subtype-specific centroids. For each TNBC6 subtype, a centroid was calculated as the mean expression profile of all samples belonging to that subtype in the TCGA dataset. These centroids represent the ‘reference signatures’ against which test samples were compared.For classification, test samples were z-transformed and compared against the TCGA-derived centroids. Correlations between each sample and all centroids were computed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Each sample was then assigned to the subtype whose centroid showed the strongest correlation with its expression profile ensuring robust classification.TNBC4-IR subtypingSubtype-specific gene sets were curated from a previous study11. Of the 719 upregulated differentially expressed genes originally reported, 217 were represented on the HTG OBP and used in TNBC4-IR subtyping. These curated gene sets served as the reference gene sets for classification. Gene expression data were log2 normalized before analysis, without additional scaling or centering. Single-sample gene set variation analysis was performed using the GSVA (v2.2.0) R package with default settings, including normalization28. Enrichment scores were calculated for each of the four subtype gene sets (BLIA, BLIS, MES and LAR), and each sample was assigned to the subtype with the highest enrichment score, taking care to avoid the ‘unclassified’ category. The workflow was implemented in R (v4.5.1) with consistent preprocessing and GSVA parameters to ensure reproducibility. Sankey plots were generated using the ggsankey package (v 0.0.99999) for TNBC subtype data for the TIL categories.Differential expression and GSEAThe normalized counts were analyzed for differential gene expression using LIMMA (v3.50.3) in R, and volcano plots were drawn for genes that were statistically significant (false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05). Briefly, for differential gene expression analysis, the normalized data were batch corrected and incorporated into the linear model fitting process using the lmFit function. Contrast matrices were applied to define the comparisons of interest, and the data were analyzed to identify differentially expressed genes. The eBayes function was used to compute t-statistics, log odds and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR-adjusted P values. All analyses were performed using default parameters unless otherwise specified.GSEA was performed using the phenotype-based method, testing predefined gene sets for enrichment across chosen group comparisons29. For GSEA, the 2,549 genes of the HTG OBP panel were mapped to 25 gene groups and pathways predefined by HTG (HTG gene sets), as well as to the established 50 hallmark gene sets of the Human Molecular Signatures Database (v2023.2) representing well-defined biological states and processes. GSEA was performed in a phenotype-based approach using normalized expression data for 2,549 genes. The analysis used 2,500 permutations, a weighted enrichment statistic and the Signal2Noise metric, with all other parameters set to default. The key metrics of GSEA enriched scores and normalized enrichment scores were scored using default conditions with few modifications (maximum size to exclude larger sets = 750, minimum size to exclude smaller sets = 10) to include all the gene sets for the analysis. The threshold for significant enrichment was defined as a P value of <0.05 and an FDR of <0.25. The statistically significant gene sets were shown as dot plots generated in R. The GSEA was carried out using Microsoft Windows-based software v.4.3.2.Statistical analysisEfficacy was evaluated in the intention-to-treat population, which included all patients who had undergone randomization. Safety was evaluated in the as-treated population, which included all patients who had undergone randomization and received at least one dose of the trial drug. The original sample size of 760 patients per arm (total 1,520 patients) was calculated for the log-rank test to provide 80% power to detect a hazard ratio of 0.70 at an overall alpha level of 0.045 with 269 EFS events. Following adjustment based on actual accrual and the decision to eliminate pCR as a co-primary endpoint, the study was redesigned to detect an HR of 0.7 for EFS between the atezolizumab arm and the placebo arm with 252 EFS confirmed events maintaining 80% power at an overall 2-sided alpha level of 0.05. However, due to consistently low monthly event rates in 2024, an amendment of the statistical analysis plan, blinded to treatment allocation, was undertaken to allow definitive analysis to proceed once at least 242 EFS events were confirmed. The resultant power loss of about 2% was considered acceptable as an alternative to waiting for 6 months based on the persistent low monthly event rate to reach 252 events. To preserve the overall level of alpha of 0.05 following the prespecified interim analysis at 196 events, which did not cross the efficacy boundary for superiority of atezolizumab, the nominal level of alpha for the final analysis of the primary endpoint calculated according to the O’Brian–Fleming boundary was 0.0444.For time-to-event endpoints, the Kaplan–Meyer product-limit method was used to present the endpoints graphically and to report 4-year rates with 95% CI. The primary comparison of EFS (and secondary time-to-event endpoints) between the two treatment arms was analyzed using the stratified log-rank test incorporating the stratification factors. To avoid over-stratification, one stratification factor PD-L1 was dropped according to the predefined strategy (if at least one stratum has fewer than four EFS events, the stratification factor that contains the level with the smallest number of patients had to be removed). The Cox proportional hazards model was used to report the HR with 95% CI between treatment arms, for the whole study population, and (without stratification) in the subgroups. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed for the subgroup analyses, including prespecified subgroup analyses, so all subgroup analyses for differential benefit of atezolizumab are (prespecified) exploratory and for the purpose of hypothesis generation. Binary endpoints (pCR) are reported as rates with 95% CI and compared between treatment arms using the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test stratified by the same factors as for the primary endpoint. An unstratified continuity-corrected chi-square test was used to compare pCR between arms in prespecified subgroups. Logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios and 95% CIs; the Breslow–Day test for interaction was used to assess heterogeneity between subgroups; for the TILs in three groups, a logistic regression with an interaction term was used.Exploratory post hoc analysis of EFS by study treatment with and without capecitabine across the entire accrual and treatment period was conducted in 557 non-pCR patients with a landmark of 6 months to avoid guarantee-time bias.
